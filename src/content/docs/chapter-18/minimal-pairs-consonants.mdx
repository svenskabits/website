A **minimal pair** is two words that differ in just one sound and have different meanings. In the pairs below, the vowel and the rest of the word stay the same while one consonant changes. Listening for that one change helps you notice distinctions that can be easy to miss when you are speaking quickly.

## Voiced and unvoiced consonants

For some consonants, the main difference is whether your vocal cords vibrate. Put a hand lightly on your throat: you can often feel vibration for **b, d, g, v**, but not for **p, t, k, f**. Swedish consonants are not identical to their English counterparts, but these pairs make the Swedish contrasts clear.

| Pair | Sound that changes | Meanings |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **pil – bil** | **p – b** | arrow – car |
| **par – bar** | **p – b** | couple – carried; also “bar” |
| **tal – dal** | **t – d** | speech / number – valley |
| **tak – tag** | **k – g** | roof – grip, as in *få tag i* (“get hold of”) |
| **god – kod** | **g – k** | good – code |
| **fin – vin** | **f – v** | fine / nice – wine |
| **får – vår** | **f – v** | sheep / gets – spring / our |
| **vila – fila** | **v – f** | rest – file, as in *fila naglarna* (“file your nails”) |

At the ends of words, the contrast still matters: **tak** ends in the unvoiced sound **k**, while **tag** ends in the voiced sound **g**. In connected speech, the next sound can affect how a consonant sounds, but it does not normally erase the word's consonant contrast.

## Sibilants: s, sj and tj

Swedish has several hissing or airy consonant sounds. The spelling gives useful clues, but it does not map one-to-one to sound. In much of central Sweden, **sj** and **sk** before a front vowel (such as **e, i, y, ä, ö**) commonly have the sj-sound; **tj** and **k** before those vowels commonly have the tj-sound. Their exact pronunciation varies by region.

| Pair | Sound that changes | Meanings |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **säl – skäl** | **s – sj** | seal – reason |
| **sil – kil** | **s – tj** | sieve – wedge |
| **skjuta – tjuta** | **sj – tj** | shoot – howl / squeal |

In **skäl**, the letters **sk** represent one consonant sound in the common pronunciation; they are not said as a separate **s** followed by a hard **k**. In **kil**, **k** before **i** represents the tj-sound. The sj-sound has several regional forms, so the contrast in **skäl – säl** may sound different from one speaker to another. Listen for the speaker's distinction rather than aiming for one exact accent.

## Other useful consonant contrasts

The same vowel-and-rest-of-word pattern also helps you hear differences between other consonants. Swedish **r** varies noticeably by region; the word pairs remain useful even when the exact r-sound changes.

| Pair | Sound that changes | Meanings |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **bär – där** | **b – d** | berry / carries – there |
| **hål – sål** | **h – s** | hole – sole of a shoe |
| **ljus – lus** | **j – l** | light – louse |
| **rik – lik** | **r – l** | rich – similar / corpse |
| **ram – lam** | **r – l** | frame – lame |
| **kam – kan** | final **m – n** | comb – can / be able to |

In **ljus**, **lj** is pronounced like **j** in the common standard pronunciation; it is not a separate **l** followed by **j**. The pair **ljus – lus** therefore contrasts the sounds at the start of the words, not the number of written letters.

## Listen for sounds, not just letters

Some words look different but are pronounced alike, so they are not consonant minimal pairs. For example, **skäl** (“reason”) and **själ** (“soul”) commonly have the same pronunciation. Likewise, **hjul** (“wheel”) and **jul** (“Christmas”) commonly begin with the same **j** sound. Spellings such as **hj**, **lj**, **gj**, and **dj** can represent a **j** sound in many everyday words.

Double consonant letters usually signal that the preceding stressed vowel is short; they do not mean that you hold the consonant for twice as long. In pairs such as **vila – villa** (“rest” – “villa”), both vowel length and consonant length differ, so this is useful spelling practice but not a minimal pair that changes only one sound. Keep that distinction in mind when you are listening for a single consonant contrast.

## Practise

1. Say each pair slowly, then at a natural pace: **pil – bil**, **tal – dal**, **god – kod**, **fin – vin**. Keep the vowel steady and change only the consonant.
2. Repeat **säl – skäl**, **sil – kil**, and **skjuta – tjuta**. Notice that **s**, the sj-sound, and the tj-sound are distinct in many Swedish accents, although the sj-sound varies regionally.
3. Try the final consonants in **kam – kan**. Keep the vowel the same and let the sound close with the lips for **m** or with the tongue for **n**.
4. Listen to a Swedish speaker say the pairs without looking at the words. Identify the sound that changes before checking the spelling and meanings.
