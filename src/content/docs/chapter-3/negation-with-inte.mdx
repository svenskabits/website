The Swedish word **inte** means **not**. In a main statement with the subject first, **inte** normally comes after the finite verb—the verb that carries the sentence's tense:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Jag arbetar inte i dag.** | I am not working today. |
| **Hon bor inte här.** | She does not live here. |
| **De kommer inte i morgon.** | They are not coming tomorrow. |

## With auxiliary and modal verbs

When a sentence has an auxiliary or modal verb followed by another verb, put **inte** after the finite auxiliary or modal and before the next verb:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Jag har inte ätit.** | I have not eaten. |
| **Han kan inte simma.** | He cannot swim. |
| **Vi vill inte vänta.** | We do not want to wait. |
| **Hon måste inte gå än.** | She does not have to leave yet. |

With **måste inte**, the usual meaning is that something is not necessary. To say that something is forbidden, use **får inte**: **Du får inte röka här.** (You must not smoke here.)

## When another word comes first

In a main clause, the finite verb usually stays in second position even when the sentence begins with a time or place phrase. **Inte** comes after the subject in these examples:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **I dag arbetar jag inte.** | Today I am not working. |
| **På måndagar går han inte till skolan.** | On Mondays he does not go to school. |

In a yes-or-no question, the verb comes before the subject, and **inte** follows the subject:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Arbetar du inte i dag?** | Aren't you working today? |
| **Kan hon inte komma?** | Can't she come? |

With a question word, use the question word first, followed by the verb and subject; **inte** normally comes after the subject: **Varför arbetar du inte i dag?** (Why are you not working today?)

## Short answers

You can use **inte** in a complete negative answer:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Arbetar du i dag? – Nej, jag arbetar inte i dag.** | Are you working today? – No, I am not working today. |
| **Kan ni komma? – Nej, vi kan inte.** | Can you come? – No, we cannot. |

## In subordinate clauses

The word order changes in a subordinate clause—a clause introduced by a word such as **att** (that), **om** (if/whether), or **eftersom** (because). In a neutral subordinate clause, **inte** usually comes before the finite verb:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Jag vet att hon inte arbetar i dag.** | I know that she is not working today. |
| **Han frågar om du inte kan komma.** | He asks whether you cannot come. |
| **Vi stannar hemma eftersom vi inte mår bra.** | We are staying home because we do not feel well. |

Compare a main clause, **Hon arbetar inte.** (She is not working.), with the subordinate clause **att hon inte arbetar** (that she is not working). The main-clause pattern puts **inte** after the finite verb; the subordinate-clause pattern normally puts it before the finite verb.

## Commands and infinitives

In a negative command, put **inte** after the imperative verb:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Vänta inte här!** | Do not wait here! |
| **Öppna inte dörren!** | Do not open the door! |

When **inte** negates an infinitive, it comes immediately before that infinitive: **Hon försöker att inte skratta.** (She is trying not to laugh.)

The main pattern to remember is: **inte** usually follows the finite verb in a main clause, but comes before the finite verb in a subordinate clause. In modal phrases, it follows the modal and comes before the infinitive.
