The letters **k** and **g** can have a soft or a hard sound in Swedish. A useful first clue is the vowel letter that comes after them.

## The basic pattern

| Letter | Before **e, i, y, ä, ö** | Before **a, o, u, å** |
|---|---|---|
| **k** | Usually a soft, hushing sound, close to the **sh** in English *she* | Usually a hard **k** sound |
| **g** | Usually a soft sound close to the **y** in English *yes* | Usually a hard **g** sound, as in English *go* |

The English comparisons are only guides: the Swedish sounds are not exactly the same. For the soft **k**, bring the tongue closer to the front of the roof of the mouth than for English **sh**. The soft **g** is voiced, like a gentle **y** sound.

The labels “soft” and “hard” describe the consonant sound, not how strongly you say it.

## Soft and hard K

Before **e, i, y, ä,** or **ö**, **k** is usually soft:

- *kemi* — chemistry
- *kilo* — kilogram
- *kyss* — kiss
- *kär* — dear; in love
- *köpa* — to buy

Before **a, o, u,** or **å**, **k** is usually hard:

- *kaka* — cake
- *ko* — cow
- *kul* — fun
- *kål* — cabbage

The same spelling clue works in the middle of words: *tacka* (to thank; female sheep) has a hard **k**, while *säker* (safe, certain) has a soft **k**.

## Soft and hard G

Before **e, i, y, ä,** or **ö**, **g** is usually soft, with a sound close to **y**:

- *ge* — to give
- *gilla* — to like
- *gyllene* — golden
- *gärna* — gladly
- *göra* — to do, to make

Before **a, o, u,** or **å**, **g** is usually hard:

- *gata* — street
- *god* — good
- *gul* — yellow
- *gå* — to go, to walk

The letter before the vowel does not change the basic clue: **g** is soft in *läger* (camp) and hard in *flagga* (flag).

## When the pattern does not tell the whole story

These are common patterns, not a guarantee for every word or name.

- **Kex** (biscuit, cracker) is pronounced with a soft **k** by many speakers and with a hard **k** by many others. Both pronunciations are established; the choice varies by speaker and region.
- In **kj**, the **k** is soft in common words such as *kjol* (skirt), even though the next vowel is **o**.
- In **gj**, the **g** is pronounced like the soft **g** in *göra*: *gjorde* (did), *gjuta* (to pour). This spelling is an exception to the vowel-letter clue.
- In **gn** at the start of a word, the **g** is usually not pronounced as a separate hard consonant: *gnista* (spark) begins with a nasal sound like the end of English *sing*, followed by **n**.
- A **g** at the end of a word does not follow the vowel clue. Its pronunciation depends on the word: compare *dag* (day), where **g** is heard, with *jag* (I) and *mig* (me), where it is often not pronounced as a separate **g** sound.
- Loanwords and names may follow different patterns, and regional pronunciation can affect the exact sound. If a word is unfamiliar, check its pronunciation rather than relying on the spelling alone.

## Practice in phrases

Read these naturally and notice which **k** and **g** sounds are soft or hard:

- *Jag gillar kaffe.* — I like coffee. The **g** in *gillar* is soft; the first **k** in *kaffe* is hard.
- *Kan du köpa mjölk?* — Can you buy milk? The **k** in *kan* is hard, the **k** in *köpa* is soft, and the final **k** in *mjölk* is hard.

When you meet **k** or **g**, look at the next vowel letter first. It gives you a helpful first guess; then learn the common exceptions and regional variants as part of each word.
