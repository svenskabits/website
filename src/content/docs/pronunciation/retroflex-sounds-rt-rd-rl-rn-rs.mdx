In much of central and northern Sweden, the letter **r** changes the sound of a following **t, d, l, n,** or **s**. Instead of saying two clearly separate consonants, speakers often blend them into one sound. These are called **retroflex sounds**: the tongue tip moves back from its usual position near the upper teeth, often with a slight curl. Do not force a deep curl; the exact tongue position varies.

## The five common spellings

The spelling stays the same even when the sounds blend. The IPA symbols below are broad descriptions of common tongue-tip pronunciations, not a requirement to copy one exact regional accent.

| Spelling | Common sound | Examples | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| **rt** | [ʈ], a voiceless stop made with the tongue tip farther back | *kort*, *svart*, *hårt* | short; black; hard (adverb) |
| **rd** | [ɖ], a voiced stop made farther back | *bord*, *jord*, *hård* | table; earth/soil; hard |
| **rl** | [ɭ], a lateral sound made farther back | *pärla*, *farlig* | pearl; dangerous |
| **rn** | [ɳ], a nasal sound made farther back | *barn*, *gärna*, *hörn* | child; gladly; corner |
| **rs** | [ʂ], a voiceless hissing sound made farther back | *först*, *kors*, *Lars* | first; cross; a man's name |

The symbols [ʈ], [ɖ], [ɭ], [ɳ], and [ʂ] describe sounds rather than spellings. The small marks on some symbols show that the tongue position is farther back than for an ordinary **t**, **d**, **l**, **n**, or **s**. In particular, **rs** is often heard as one continuous sound rather than a clear **r** followed by **s**.

## Listen for the blend

Try saying the examples smoothly, without putting a pause or an extra vowel between the letters:

- *Ett kort ligger på bordet.* — A card is on the table.
- *Barnen tycker om pärlor.* — The children like pearls.
- *Det är svårt att gå först.* — It is difficult to go first.
- *Jag hör gärna sorlet.* — I like hearing the murmur.

The first examples make the spellings easy to notice, but everyday speech is not always perfectly regular. Listen for the tongue moving back as the consonants meet, rather than trying to make a separate **r** and then a separate consonant.

## Where the pattern occurs

The pattern is common inside words, including across syllables or parts of a word: *pärla* has **rl**, *gärna* has **rn**, and *hård* has **rd**. It can also happen across a word boundary when one word ends in **r** and the next begins with **t, d, l, n,** or **s**. How much blending you hear across words depends on the speaker, region, and speaking style; clear separate sounds are useful while you are learning.

This is a pronunciation pattern, not a spelling change. Keep writing **r** and the following consonant. A written sequence does not by itself guarantee a retroflex sound in every speaker's pronunciation.

## Regional and speaker variation

Retroflex sounds are especially associated with Swedish varieties that use a tongue-tip **r**. In many parts of southern Sweden, **r** is made farther back in the mouth, near the uvula. Speakers with this kind of **r** often do not make the same tongue-tip retroflex sounds; the **r** and following consonant may remain more distinct or combine in another way. There is variation within regions too, and speakers may pronounce the same word somewhat differently.

The [ʂ] sound in **rs** is not identical for every speaker. Its quality may vary, and it may sound somewhat like the sound commonly spelled **sj** to some listeners. Do not assume the two are always identical: both sounds vary regionally, and their relationship depends on the speaker. These differences are normal, not mistakes.

## Practice

Read each group slowly first, then say it at a natural pace. Notice that the spelling gives you a useful clue, while the spoken result depends on the speaker's variety of Swedish.

- *kort – hårt – svart* — short/card – hard (adverb) – black
- *bord – jord – hård* — table – earth/soil – hard
- *pärla – farlig* — pearl – dangerous
- *barn – gärna – hörn* — child – gladly – corner
- *först – kors – Lars* — first – cross – Lars

When listening to Swedish, try to recognize both a blended tongue-tip version and regional versions where **r** sounds different. Aim for clear, relaxed speech rather than forcing a particular tongue shape.
