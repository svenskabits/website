In Swedish, a doubled consonant letter is often a clue that the stressed vowel before it is short. The spelling also helps show a sound contrast: a long vowel is usually followed by a short consonant, while a short vowel is often followed by a long consonant or a group of consonants.

## A short vowel and a long consonant

Compare these words. In each pair, the spelling and the length pattern change the meaning:

| Long vowel + short consonant | Short vowel + long consonant | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *vila* | *villa* | to rest; villa / house |
| *mata* | *matta* | to feed; rug |
| *hat* | *hatt* | hatred; hat |
| *glas* | *glass* | drinking glass; ice cream |
| *tak* | *tack* | roof; thanks / please |

For example, *vila* has a long **i** and a short **l**, while *villa* has a short **i** and a longer **l**. Say the consonant in *villa* as one held sound, not as two separate **l** sounds with a pause between them. With a stop consonant such as **t** or **k**, the longer sound is mainly a longer closure in the mouth, not two separate releases.

## How the spelling gives you a clue

In a stressed syllable, these are useful patterns:

- A vowel before one consonant letter is often long: *vila*, *hata* (to hate), *tak* (roof).
- A vowel before a doubled consonant is usually short: *villa*, *hatta* (to land), *tack* (thanks).
- A vowel before a consonant cluster—two or more different consonants together—is also usually short: *katt* (cat), *fisk* (fish), *tända* (to light / switch on).

The doubled letter does not normally mean that you pronounce the consonant twice. It signals the short-vowel/long-consonant pattern. A single consonant letter can also be pronounced long when it is next to another consonant in a cluster, so do not expect every short vowel to be followed by a doubled letter.

## The pattern between syllables

When one consonant sound comes between a stressed vowel and the next vowel, Swedish spelling often doubles its letter if the stressed vowel is short:

| Short vowel + doubled consonant | Long vowel + single consonant | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *vissa* | *visa* | certain / some; to show, or song |
| *villa* | *vila* | villa / house; to rest |
| *matta* | *mata* | rug; to feed |

This makes the spelling useful even when you do not know a word. It is a strong pattern, not a guarantee for every word. In *flicka* (girl), for instance, the short **i** is followed by the consonant cluster **ck**, not by a doubled single letter.

## Word endings and word forms

Doubled consonants also occur at the end of words, as in *tack*, *katt* (cat), and *sill* (herring). They remain when a vowel-ending form is added: *katten* (the cat), *sillen* (the herring), *hatten* (the hat). This spelling helps preserve the short vowel and long consonant pattern.

An ending can also create a doubled consonant. For example, an **-t** ending on an adjective may give **tt**: *vit* (white) becomes *vitt* (white, with an **ett** noun), and *röd* (red) becomes *rött*. The vowel in *vitt* and *rött* is short. The doubled letter is part of the written form; pronounce one held consonant, not two separate ones.

## A practical way to read a new word

First find the stressed syllable. Then look at the vowel and the consonants that follow it. One consonant letter often points to a long vowel; a doubled letter or a consonant cluster often points to a short vowel. Listen to the word as a whole too: vowel and consonant length work together, and spelling is a helpful guide rather than a perfect pronunciation key.

- *Hatten är svart.* — The hat is black. (*a* is short before **tt**.)
- *Jag vill vila.* — I want to rest. (*i* in *vila* is long.)
- *Vi har en villa.* — We have a villa / house. (*i* is short before **ll**.)
- *Tack för maten!* — Thanks for the food! (*a* in *tack* is short.)

The exact timing can vary somewhat between speakers and regions. The important contrast in standard Swedish is the pattern: stressed long vowels generally pair with shorter consonants, and stressed short vowels generally pair with longer consonants or consonant clusters.
