A Swedish tongue twister repeats sounds that are easy to mix up when you speak quickly. It is a practice tool, not a model of ordinary fast speech: start slowly, make each word clear, and increase the pace only while you can still say the sounds accurately. A tongue twister can be difficult even for a native speaker.

## Repeated sje-sounds

Try this familiar, deliberately playful sentence:

> **Sju sjösjuka sjömän sköttes av sju sköna sjuksköterskor på det sjunkande skeppet.**
>
> Seven seasick sailors were cared for by seven lovely nurses on the sinking ship.

The repeated sound appears with two spellings: **sj** in **sju**, **sjösjuka**, **sjuksköterskor**, and **sjunkande**; and **sk** before a front vowel in **sköttes**, **sköna**, and **skeppet**. In standard Swedish in Sweden, **sj** and soft **sk** can represent the sje-sound. Its exact quality varies across regions and speakers. Do not assume that every spelling of **sk** has this sound: **sk** before a back vowel, as in **ska**, normally has a hard k sound.

Say the sentence in short groups first: **sju sjösjuka sjömän**; **sköttes av sju sköna sjuksköterskor**; **på det sjunkande skeppet**. Keep the repeated sound steady instead of changing it to an English **sh** automatically; English **sh** is only a close starting point for some speakers, not an exact match for every Swedish variety.

## S and x in a short phrase

> **Sex laxar i en laxask.**
>
> Six salmon in a salmon box.

The phrase puts **s** at the start of **sex**, **laxar**, and **laxask**, and repeats the **x** spelling in **sex** and **lax**. In these words, **x** is pronounced like **ks**, not like the sound in English *xylophone*. Join the words smoothly, but keep the final consonants clear: **sex laxar** has a short **ks** at the end of **sex**, followed immediately by **l**.

## P, k, and consonant length

> **Packa pappas kappsäck.**
>
> Pack Dad's suitcase.

The repeated **p** and **k** sounds make this short instruction tricky. In **packa**, **ck** represents a long **k** after a short vowel; the doubled **pp** in **pappas** and **kappsäck** signals a long **p**. A long stop consonant has a longer closure, not an extra vowel between the sounds. Keep the words distinct enough to hear **packa**, **pappas**, and **kappsäck**, while linking them into one phrase.

## F, l, and changing vowels

> **Fly, fula fluga, fly! Och den fula flugan flög.**
>
> Fly, ugly fly, fly! And the ugly fly flew.

Repeat the **f** and **l** without letting them disappear, and notice how the vowel changes in **fluga** and **flög**. The letters **u** and **ö** represent different Swedish vowels: do not replace them with the same vowel just to speak faster. You can practise one part at a time: **fula fluga**, **den fula flugan**, then the whole phrase.

## Make practice useful

1. Read the phrase silently and check any unfamiliar word or meaning.
2. Say it slowly in word groups. Make the consonants and vowels clear; do not insert extra vowels into clusters.
3. Repeat it at a comfortable pace, keeping the same sounds and word stress.
4. Increase the speed gradually. Stop increasing it if a sound changes or a word becomes unclear.
5. Record yourself or ask a Swedish speaker to listen. Compare the sounds and rhythm, not just the number of repetitions per minute.

Regional differences are normal, especially for sounds such as the sje-sound. Use a consistent pronunciation that is comfortable for you, and treat a tongue twister as a way to notice and practise sound sequences—not as a test of whether your accent is correct.
