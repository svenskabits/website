Use **fast**, **trots att**, and **även om** to connect two ideas when one seems surprising in view of the other. They can all be translated with words such as “although,” “though,” or “even though,” but they are not always interchangeable.

## Fast: although or though

As a conjunction, **fast** introduces a contrast. It is common in everyday Swedish and can be used in speech and writing:

| Swedish | English |
| --- | --- |
| Jag gick till jobbet fast jag var sjuk. | I went to work even though I was ill. |
| Hon tycker om kaffe fast hon inte dricker det så ofta. | She likes coffee, although she doesn’t drink it very often. |
| Det var dyrt, fast kvaliteten var bra. | It was expensive, though the quality was good. |

In this use, **fast** introduces a subordinate clause. In that clause, **inte** normally comes before the finite verb (the verb that carries the tense): **fast hon inte dricker**. This is the usual subordinate-clause word order.

Do not confuse this conjunction with the adjective **fast**, meaning “firm,” “fixed,” or “solid,” as in *en fast tjänst* (“a permanent job”) or *marken är fast* (“the ground is firm”). The meaning is clear from the sentence.

## Trots att: despite the fact that

**Trots att** means “despite the fact that” or “even though.” It presents the following information as a fact that does not prevent the result:

| Swedish | English |
| --- | --- |
| Trots att det regnade gick vi ut. | Even though it was raining, we went out. |
| Han klarade provet trots att han var nervös. | He passed the test despite being nervous. |
| De fortsatte att spela trots att det blev mörkt. | They kept playing even though it was getting dark. |

**Trots att** introduces a subordinate clause, so subordinate-clause word order applies:

| Swedish | English |
| --- | --- |
| Vi åkte trots att vi inte hade någon bil. | We went even though we didn’t have a car. |
| Trots att hon inte kände någon stannade hon länge. | Even though she didn’t know anyone, she stayed for a long time. |

When the subordinate clause comes first, the verb in the main clause comes before its subject. This is called inversion: **Trots att … stannade hon**, not *stannade* after **hon**.

Use **trots** without **att** before a noun or noun phrase, not a full clause:

| Swedish | English |
| --- | --- |
| Trots regnet gick vi ut. | Despite the rain, we went out. |
| Trots sin trötthet fortsatte hon att arbeta. | Despite her tiredness, she continued working. |

Compare **trots regnet** (“despite the rain”) with **trots att det regnade** (“despite the fact that it was raining”).

## Även om: even though or even if

**Även om** can introduce a concession about something that is true, or a condition that may or may not happen. Its English translation depends on the context.

When the information is known or presented as true, it means “even though” or “although”:

| Swedish | English |
| --- | --- |
| Även om jag är trött vill jag följa med. | Even though I’m tired, I want to come along. |
| Hon log även om hon var besviken. | She smiled even though she was disappointed. |

When the clause describes a possible or imagined situation, it means “even if”:

| Swedish | English |
| --- | --- |
| Även om det regnar går vi till stranden. | Even if it rains, we’re going to the beach. |
| Jag skulle inte köpa huset även om jag hade råd. | I wouldn’t buy the house even if I could afford it. |

The grammar is the same in both meanings: **även om** introduces a subordinate clause, and **inte** comes before the finite verb in that clause: **även om jag inte har tid** (“even if I don’t have time”).

## Choosing between them

Use **fast** for a straightforward, often conversational contrast. **Trots att** clearly presents something as a fact that did not stop the other event. **Även om** works for a factual concession too, but it is especially useful for a possible or imagined condition (“even if”).

| Swedish | English |
| --- | --- |
| Fast jag var trött gick jag upp tidigt. | Though I was tired, I got up early. |
| Trots att jag var trött gick jag upp tidigt. | Despite being tired, I got up early. |
| Även om jag är trött går jag upp tidigt. | Even though I’m tired, I get up early. |
| Även om jag är trött i morgon går jag upp tidigt. | Even if I’m tired tomorrow, I’ll get up early. |

The distinctions are not absolute: in a factual contrast, **fast**, **trots att**, and **även om** can sometimes describe the same situation. Choose the expression that best matches the emphasis and whether the situation is a fact or a possibility.

## Word order when the clause comes first

Any of these subordinate clauses can come before or after the main clause. If it comes first, the main clause has inversion: the verb comes before the subject.

| Subordinate clause first | Main clause first |
| --- | --- |
| Fast det var sent gick vi hem. — Although it was late, we went home. | Vi gick hem fast det var sent. — We went home although it was late. |
| Trots att hon var sjuk gick hon till jobbet. — Even though she was ill, she went to work. | Hon gick till jobbet trots att hon var sjuk. — She went to work even though she was ill. |
| Även om det blir kallt stannar vi ute. — Even if it gets cold, we’ll stay outside. | Vi stannar ute även om det blir kallt. — We’ll stay outside even if it gets cold. |

The subordinate clauses follow the same word order in each case. For example, say **även om det inte blir kallt** (“even if it doesn’t get cold”), with **inte** before **blir**. In a main clause, the order is different: **Det blir inte kallt** (“It isn’t getting cold”).
