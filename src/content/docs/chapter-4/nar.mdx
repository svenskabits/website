**När** can mean **when** in two related ways: it can ask about a time, or it can introduce a clause that tells when something happens. The word order depends on how **när** is used.

## När in a direct question

In a direct question, **när** comes first, followed by the finite verb and then the subject:

**när + verb + subject + the rest?**

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **När börjar filmen?** | When does the film start? |
| **När kommer du hem?** | When are you coming home? |
| **När kan vi äta?** | When can we eat? |

The verb comes before the subject, and Swedish does not add English-style *do* or *does*.

## När in an indirect question

An indirect question is part of a larger sentence. After **när**, put the subject before the verb:

| Direct question | Indirect question | English |
|---|---|---|
| **När börjar filmen?** | **Jag vet när filmen börjar.** | When does the film start? / I know when the film starts. |
| **När kommer bussen?** | **Vet du när bussen kommer?** | When is the bus coming? / Do you know when the bus is coming? |

In **Vet du** (Do you know?), the whole sentence is a direct question, so its verb comes before its subject. Inside it, **när bussen kommer** (when the bus is coming) is an indirect question, so **bussen** comes before **kommer**. An indirect question inside a statement ends with a period; the whole sentence ends with a question mark if it is itself a direct question.

## När as a time conjunction

**När** can also mean *when* or *whenever* and join a time clause to a main clause. A time clause is a subordinate clause—it cannot normally stand alone as a complete sentence. In this clause, use ordinary subordinate-clause order: **när + subject + verb**.

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Jag ringer dig när jag kommer hem.** | I will call you when I get home. |
| **Vi äter när pappa kommer hem.** | We eat when Dad comes home. |
| **Hon blir glad när du ringer.** | She is happy when you call. |

Swedish commonly uses the present tense in a **när** time clause even when the meaning is future: **när jag kommer hem** (when I get home), not a future-tense form.

The time clause can come before the main clause. Then the main clause still follows Swedish verb-second word order: its verb comes before its subject. A comma is commonly used after the opening clause:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **När filmen slutar, går vi hem.** | When the film ends, we will go home. |
| **När jag kommer hem, lagar jag middag.** | When I get home, I will make dinner. |

Compare **När jag kommer hem** (when I get home), with subject before verb inside the time clause, and **går vi hem** (we will go home), with the main-clause verb before its subject because the time clause comes first. If the main clause comes first, no comma is normally needed before the time clause: **Vi går hem när filmen slutar.** (We will go home when the film ends.)

## Tell the uses apart

An indirect question asks for information about the time: **Jag vet när filmen börjar.** (I know when the film starts.) A time clause tells when another event happens: **Jag äter middag när jag kommer hem.** (I eat dinner when I get home.) In both, the clause after **när** has subject-before-verb order; the meaning and the larger sentence show which use it is.

Remember: use verb-before-subject order in a direct **när** question. In an indirect question or time clause, put the subject before the verb. When a time clause comes first, the following main clause has verb-before-subject order.
