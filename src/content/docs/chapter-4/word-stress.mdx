In a word with more than one syllable, one syllable usually stands out as the main one. This is **word stress**. The stressed syllable is often clearer and more prominent, and may be longer or have a pitch movement. Do not rely on loudness alone: keep the other syllables natural and unstressed.

In the examples below, **bold** marks the main stressed syllable.

## Finding the stressed syllable

Many common Swedish words have stress on the first syllable:

- **som**mar — summer
- **flick**a — girl
- **lör**dag — Saturday
- **kö**per — buys, is buying

But stress is part of how a word is learned; spelling does not always tell you where it falls. Some familiar words have stress later in the word:

- tele**fon** — telephone
- ba**nan** — banana
- ca**fé** — café

Listen to a new word and remember its stress along with its meaning. A dictionary pronunciation guide or a recording can help when the stress is not obvious.

## Stress can distinguish words

The same spelling can have different meanings when the stress changes. For example:

| Word and stress | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ba**nan** | the banana |
| **ba**nan | the track, as in a running track; the definite form of *bana* (track) |

The sounds and meanings help you tell the words apart in context. In ordinary speech, speakers do not usually pronounce the syllables as separate beats; keep the word flowing while giving the stressed syllable more prominence.

## Stress in compounds and longer words

In a Swedish compound, the first part often has the main stress. A later part may have a weaker, secondary stress:

- **fot**boll — football, soccer
- **som**marstuga — summer cottage

This is a useful tendency, not a rule for every compound or every long word. Some compounds have less obvious stress, and words borrowed from other languages may keep a different pattern. Learn the stress of the complete word rather than trying to calculate it from the number of syllables.

Endings such as the plural or definite endings are often unstressed, so the main stress stays on the base of many familiar words: **flick**a → **flick**or (girl → girls); **bil** → **bil**en (car → the car). This is common, but stress can vary between related words, so listen to each form.

## Word stress and the sound of a sentence

Word stress belongs to the word. In a sentence, a speaker can also emphasize a whole word to show contrast or importance. For example, in *Jag köper kaffe* (I am buying coffee), the speaker might emphasize *köper* (buying) to contrast it with selling, or *kaffe* (coffee) to contrast it with tea. This sentence emphasis does not normally move the usual syllable stress: **kö**per and **kaf**fe.

Swedish also has pitch patterns associated with many words, sometimes called word accents. They are a melody across syllables, separate from which syllable has word stress. Their use and sound vary by word and by region. For an A1 learner, first notice the stressed syllable; you do not need to assign a pitch pattern from the spelling.

## Practice

Say the phrases at a natural pace. Give the bold syllables a little more prominence, without making the other syllables disappear:

- Jag **spe**lar **fot**boll. — I play football (soccer).
- Vi **lyss**nar på mu**sik**. — We listen to music.
- Hon **ring**er på **lör**dag. — She is calling on Saturday.

Stress and exact pitch can sound different across regions and speakers. These differences are normal. Focus on learning the usual stressed syllable of each word and keeping the rest of the word smooth.
