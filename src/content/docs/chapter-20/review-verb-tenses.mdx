Swedish verbs show when an event happens and how it relates to another time. The main forms to recognize are the **present**, **preterite** (simple past), **perfect**, and **pluperfect**. Swedish does not have a special future-tense ending: speakers use the present tense or constructions with verbs such as **ska** and **kommer**.

## The main forms at a glance

The verb form itself normally stays the same for every subject. For example, **arbetar** works with *jag*, *du*, *hon*, and *de*. The perfect and pluperfect use an auxiliary verb plus the **supine**, a form used after **har** and **hade**.

| Time and form | Pattern | Example | English |
|---|---|---|---|
| Present | present form | **Jag arbetar hemma.** | I work at home. / I am working at home. |
| Preterite | preterite form | **Jag arbetade hemma i går.** | I worked at home yesterday. |
| Perfect | **har** + supine | **Jag har arbetat hemma i dag.** | I have worked at home today. |
| Pluperfect | **hade** + supine | **Jag hade arbetat hemma innan jag flyttade.** | I had worked at home before I moved. |
| Future (one common pattern) | **ska** + infinitive | **Jag ska arbeta hemma i morgon.** | I am going to work at home tomorrow. |

The infinitive is the dictionary form, such as **arbeta** (to work). The supine is **arbetat**. Many supines end in **-t**, but the form is not always predictable, so learn it with the verb.

| Infinitive | Present | Preterite | Supine |
|---|---|---|---|
| **arbeta** (work) | **arbetar** | **arbetade** | **arbetat** |
| **köpa** (buy) | **köper** | **köpte** | **köpt** |
| **bo** (live) | **bor** | **bodde** | **bott** |
| **vara** (be) | **är** | **var** | **varit** |
| **gå** (go, walk) | **går** | **gick** | **gått** |
| **skriva** (write) | **skriver** | **skrev** | **skrivit** |

## Present: now, habits, and general facts

The present form describes something happening now, something that happens regularly, or a general fact. Swedish uses the same form where English may use either the simple present or the present progressive (*am working*):

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Jag läser en rapport just nu.** | I am reading a report right now. |
| **Hon arbetar på ett bibliotek.** | She works at a library. |
| **Vatten kokar vid hundra grader.** | Water boils at one hundred degrees. |

For a repeated or customary activity, **brukar** + infinitive is common:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Vi brukar äta lunch vid tolv.** | We usually eat lunch at twelve. |
| **Han brukar cykla till jobbet.** | He usually cycles to work. |

The present can also refer to the future when a future time is clear, especially for arrangements and schedules: **Tåget går klockan nio i morgon.** (The train leaves at nine tomorrow.)

## Preterite: events and situations in finished past time

Use the preterite for events or situations presented as belonging to a past time. It works for a single event, a repeated past habit, or a past state:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Vi träffades i Uppsala förra veckan.** | We met in Uppsala last week. |
| **När han var liten spelade han piano varje dag.** | When he was little, he played the piano every day. |
| **Det snöade hela natten.** | It snowed all night. |

Swedish has no separate verb ending for an action that was in progress in the past. The preterite can give the background to another event, much like English *was reading*:

> **Jag lagade middag när du ringde.** — I was making dinner when you called.

To emphasize that someone was in the middle of an action, use **höll på att** + infinitive:

> **Jag höll på att laga middag när du ringde.** — I was in the middle of making dinner when you called.

For a past habit, **brukade** + infinitive is another option:

> **Vi brukade bada där varje sommar.** — We used to swim there every summer.

## Perfect: past connected to the present

Form the perfect with **har** and the supine. **Har** is the same with every subject, and the supine does not change:

> **De har skrivit ett nytt förslag.** — They have written a new proposal.

The perfect commonly presents an experience up to now, a present result, an event in a period that is still open, or a situation that began earlier and continues:

| Meaning | Swedish | English |
|---|---|---|
| Experience | **Har du sett den här filmen?** | Have you seen this film? |
| Result that matters now | **Jag har tappat mina nycklar.** | I have lost my keys. |
| Period continuing to now | **Det har regnat mycket i år.** | It has rained a lot this year. |
| Situation continuing to now | **Hon har bott här sedan 2020.** | She has lived here since 2020. |

With a continuing situation, **sedan** means “since” and **i** can mean “for”: **Vi har känt varandra i tio år.** (We have known each other for ten years.)

## Preterite or perfect?

The choice depends on how the speaker frames the time, not on a one-to-one match with English. The preterite places an event in a finished past occasion; the perfect connects it to now or leaves the exact past time unstated:

| Preterite: a finished past occasion | Perfect: experience or connection to now |
|---|---|
| **Jag såg henne i går.** — I saw her yesterday. | **Jag har sett henne förut.** — I have seen her before. |
| **Vi bodde i Lund förra året.** — We lived in Lund last year. | **Vi har bott i Lund i tre år.** — We have lived in Lund for three years. |

Expressions such as **i går** (yesterday) and **förra året** (last year) commonly go with the preterite because they name a finished time. An open period such as **i dag** (today) or **i år** (this year) often goes with the perfect. These are clues, not automatic rules: **Jag träffade henne i dag klockan tio.** (I met her today at ten.) uses the preterite because the event is presented as a completed occasion at a definite time.

## Pluperfect: before a point in the past

Form the pluperfect with **hade** + supine. Use it for something that had happened or had been true before a past reference point:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **När vi kom fram hade filmen redan börjat.** | When we arrived, the film had already started. |
| **Hon kände sig hemma eftersom hon hade bott där länge.** | She felt at home because she had lived there for a long time. |

The earlier event takes **hade** + supine; the later past event often uses the preterite. If the order is already clear, the preterite may also be used for both events: **Efter att hon stängde dörren gick hon.** (After she closed the door, she left.)

Compare the three past forms:

| Form | Swedish | English |
|---|---|---|
| Preterite | **Jag åt innan de kom.** | I ate before they arrived. |
| Perfect | **Jag har ätit.** | I have eaten. |
| Pluperfect | **Jag hade ätit innan de kom.** | I had eaten before they arrived. |

## Talking about the future

Swedish has no single inflected future form. The present tense and several auxiliary constructions express future time; the choice depends on whether the speaker means an arrangement, intention, expectation, or prediction.

| Construction | Typical use | Swedish | English |
|---|---|---|---|
| Present + future time | Arrangement or schedule | **Vi åker på fredag.** | We are leaving on Friday. |
| **ska** + infinitive | Plan, intention, or decision | **Jag ska ringa henne i kväll.** | I am going to call her this evening. |
| **kommer att** + infinitive | Expectation or prediction | **Det kommer att bli kallt i natt.** | It will get cold tonight. |

These meanings overlap, so they are tendencies rather than strict rules. A future time expression often makes the time clear. Without one, a present-tense sentence is normally understood as happening now or regularly.

To describe something that will be completed before a future point, use **ska ha** or **kommer att ha** + supine:

> **På fredag ska jag ha lämnat in rapporten.** — By Friday, I will have submitted the report.

## A future viewed from the past

**Skulle** + infinitive can describe a plan or event that was still in the future from a past point of view. It can mean “was/were going to” or “would,” depending on context:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Hon sa att hon skulle ringa senare.** | She said that she would call later. |
| **Vi skulle flytta i juni, men planerna ändrades.** | We were going to move in June, but the plans changed. |

For an unreal result in the past, use **skulle ha** + supine. In a condition about an unreal past event, the **om** clause commonly uses **hade** + supine:

> **Om jag hade vetat det, skulle jag ha ringt dig.** — If I had known that, I would have called you.

Here **hade** + supine marks the unreal past condition and **skulle ha** + supine marks its imagined result.

## Word order in verb phrases

The tense does not remove the usual Swedish word-order rules. In a main clause, the finite verb—the verb that carries tense—comes second. If another element comes first, the subject follows the finite verb:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Jag har inte läst mejlet.** | I have not read the email. |
| **I morse läste jag mejlet.** | This morning I read the email. |
| **I morse hade jag redan läst mejlet.** | This morning I had already read the email. |

In a main clause, **inte** usually comes after the finite verb: **Hon kommer inte att arbeta i morgon.** (She will not work tomorrow.) In a subordinate clause, it usually comes before the finite verb: **Jag vet att hon inte kommer att arbeta i morgon.** (I know that she will not work tomorrow.)

In questions, put the finite verb before the subject: **Har du redan ätit?** (Have you eaten already?) The same applies when a question word comes first: **När hade de kommit hem?** (When had they come home?)

## Choosing a form

When you are unsure, ask:

1. Is this a present habit, general fact, or action happening now? Use the present; use **brukar** for a customary action.
2. Am I placing it in a finished past time? Use the preterite.
3. Is it a past experience or event connected to now? Use **har** + supine.
4. Did it happen before another past time? Use **hade** + supine if that earlier timing needs to be clear.
5. Is it future? Use the present with a clear future time, **ska** for a plan, or **kommer att** for an expectation or prediction.

Time expressions help, but context and the speaker's point of view determine which form sounds right.
