Intonation is the way your voice moves up and down across a phrase or sentence. It helps a listener hear whether you are making a statement, asking a question, continuing a thought, or emphasizing a particular piece of information. The examples below show common patterns in standard Swedish, but they are guides rather than fixed rules.

## Statements often fall at the end

In a neutral statement, the voice commonly moves down toward the end. Keep the sentence flowing, then let the final prominent part settle lower:

- *Vi ses på lördag.* — See you on Saturday.
- *Jag bor i Göteborg.* — I live in Gothenburg.
- *Hon kommer senare.* — She is coming later.

The fall does not mean the speaker is angry or definite. It is a common way to make an ordinary statement sound complete.

## Yes/no questions often rise

Questions that can be answered with *ja* or *nej* often have a rising movement near the end. The rise signals that the speaker is inviting an answer:

- *Ska du följa med?* — Are you coming along?
- *Vill du ha kaffe?* — Would you like coffee?
- *Är bussen här?* — Is the bus here?

You do not need to make the whole sentence rise. Keep the earlier words natural and let the voice lift on the final prominent part. The rise may be gentle or more noticeable. A yes/no question can also have a flatter or falling contour depending on the speaker, context, and intention, so do not treat a rise as a compulsory rule.

## Wh-questions often fall

Questions beginning with words such as *vad* (what), *var* (where), *när* (when), and *hur* (how) commonly end with a fall, like a statement:

- *Vad ska du göra i helgen?* — What are you doing this weekend?
- *Var ligger stationen?* — Where is the station?
- *När börjar filmen?* — When does the film start?

The question word already makes it clear that information is being requested, so a strong final rise is not usually needed. A rise may still occur when the speaker is surprised, checking what they heard, or asking for clarification.

## Keep a list open; let the last item finish

When you name several things, the voice can rise or stay somewhat open on items that are not last, then fall on the final item. This helps the listener hear that the list is continuing and then ending:

- *Vi behöver bröd, mjölk och äpplen.* — We need bread, milk, and apples.
- *På lördag kan vi fika, promenera eller gå på bio.* — On Saturday we can have coffee, go for a walk, or go to the cinema.

The movement on each item can be subtle. Do not pause for a long time between every item; use a small pause or a light continuation in the voice.

## Emphasis changes what stands out

Intonation can draw attention to the part of a sentence that is new or important. A more prominent pitch movement, together with stress, can highlight a word. The same sentence can therefore answer different questions:

- **JAG** ska gå på bio. — *I* am going to the cinema. (I am, not someone else.)
- Jag ska gå på **BIO**. — I am going to the *cinema*. (To the cinema, not somewhere else.)

These are examples of contrastive emphasis, not a requirement to shout or to use an exaggerated melody. In ordinary speech, context often makes the intended emphasis clear, and the pitch movement may be small.

## Punctuation is a clue, not a tune

A question mark does not always mean that the voice must rise. Many information questions (*Var bor du?*) commonly fall, while a statement can rise if the speaker is checking something or expressing uncertainty:

- *Du kommer på lördag?* — You're coming on Saturday? (checking or confirming)

Likewise, a rising tune does not by itself turn every sentence into a question. Listeners use the words, word order, context, and intonation together.

## Practise the contrast

Say each pair with the same words, changing only the intention and the voice movement. Use a gentle movement, not a dramatic one:

- *Du ska följa med.* — You are coming along. (neutral statement, commonly falling)
- *Ska du följa med?* — Are you coming along? (yes/no question, commonly rising)
- *Var ska vi ses?* — Where shall we meet? (wh-question, commonly falling)
- *Du ska följa med?* — You're coming along? (checking or surprised; a rise is possible)

Swedish speakers differ in their exact melodies. Regional accent, speaking style, emotion, and the situation all affect pitch. Swedish also has pitch patterns within individual words; those word-level patterns are separate from the larger movement that helps a sentence sound complete or questioning. Aim first for a clear, relaxed contrast between a complete statement, an open yes/no question, and a wh-question.
