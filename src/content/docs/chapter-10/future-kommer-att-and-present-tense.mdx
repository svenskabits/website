Swedish does not have one special verb form that is used only for the future. Speakers commonly use **kommer att** with an infinitive, **ska** for plans and intentions, or the present tense with a future time expression. The choice depends on how the speaker presents the event.

## Kommer att + infinitive

Use **kommer att** followed by the infinitive (the dictionary form) of a verb to talk about something expected or predicted to happen. This is especially common for predictions, forecasts, and future developments. **Kommer** has the same form with every subject.

| Subject | Example | English |
|---|---|---|
| jag (I) | Jag **kommer att** bo här länge. | I will live here for a long time. |
| du (you) | Du **kommer att** förstå snart. | You will understand soon. |
| han (he) | Han **kommer att** bli en bra lärare. | He will become a good teacher. |
| vi (we) | Vi **kommer att** få mer information i morgon. | We will get more information tomorrow. |
| de (they) | De **kommer att** resa senare. | They will travel later. |

The verb after **att** stays in the infinitive. It does not change to match the subject:

> Det kommer att regna i kväll. <br />
> It will rain this evening.

> Priserna kommer att öka nästa år. <br />
> Prices will rise next year.

> Hon kommer att vara hemma efter klockan sex. <br />
> She will be at home after six o'clock.

**Kommer att** often presents the future as an expectation or prediction, rather than as someone's chosen plan. It is not a strict rule: the context determines the meaning, and predictions can be uncertain.

> Jag tror att det kommer att bli kallt i natt. <br />
> I think it will get cold tonight.

> Om du fortsätter att öva kommer du att bli bättre. <br />
> If you keep practising, you will get better.

## Word order with kommer att

In a statement, put the subject first, then **kommer**, then **att** and the infinitive:

> Bussen kommer att komma om fem minuter. <br />
> The bus will arrive in five minutes.

In a main clause, **inte** comes after the finite verb **kommer** and before **att**:

> Jag kommer inte att arbeta på lördag. <br />
> I will not work on Saturday.

> Det kommer inte att ta lång tid. <br />
> It will not take long.

For a yes/no question, put **kommer** before the subject:

> Kommer ni att stanna här? <br />
> Will you stay here?

With a question word, put the question word first, followed by **kommer** and the subject:

> När kommer tåget att vara framme? <br />
> When will the train arrive?

> Vad kommer du att göra efter kursen? <br />
> What will you do after the course?

In a subordinate clause after words such as **att** (“that”), **om** (“if/whether”), and **eftersom** (“because”), **inte** normally comes before **kommer**:

> Jag tror att hon inte kommer att hinna i dag. <br />
> I think she will not have time today.

> Vi stannar hemma eftersom det inte kommer att sluta regna före kvällen. <br />
> We will stay at home because it will not stop raining before the evening.

## Present tense for a future event

The Swedish present tense can refer to the future when a future time is clear from the context. This is especially natural for events that are arranged, planned, or part of a schedule. The verb keeps its ordinary present-tense form; the time expression or situation tells us that the event is in the future.

> Jag träffar Sara i morgon. <br />
> I'm meeting Sara tomorrow.

> Vi åker till Malmö på fredag. <br />
> We're going to Malmö on Friday.

> Filmen börjar klockan sju i kväll. <br />
> The film starts at seven this evening.

> De öppnar den nya butiken nästa vecka. <br />
> They open the new shop next week.

Without a future time expression or a clear context, a present-tense sentence is usually understood as happening now or regularly, not in the future:

> Jag arbetar på lördag. <br />
> I'm working on Saturday.

Here, **på lördag** makes the future meaning clear. Compare:

> Jag arbetar på lördagar. <br />
> I work on Saturdays.

The present tense is particularly common for timetables and fixed schedules:

> Tåget går klockan 8.15 i morgon. <br />
> The train leaves at 8:15 tomorrow.

> Butiken stänger tidigare på julafton. <br />
> The shop closes earlier on Christmas Eve.

It is also natural for personal arrangements. A future time expression is often included to prevent confusion:

> Vi äter middag med mina föräldrar på söndag. <br />
> We're having dinner with my parents on Sunday.

> Jag börjar mitt nya jobb den första juni. <br />
> I start my new job on the first of June.

## Choosing between the forms

These forms can describe the same future event, but they emphasize different things:

| Form | Typical use | Example | English |
|---|---|---|---|
| **kommer att** + infinitive | Prediction or expectation | Det kommer att snöa i morgon. | It will snow tomorrow. |
| Present tense + future time | Arrangement, schedule, or fixed event | Vi åker klockan nio i morgon. | We're leaving at nine tomorrow. |

Compare a prediction with an arrangement:

> Det kommer att bli varmt i helgen. <br />
> It will be warm this weekend. *(a prediction)*

> Vi badar i sjön på lördag. <br />
> We're swimming in the lake on Saturday. *(an arrangement or plan)*

For a person's intention or a decision about what they are going to do, **ska** is often the natural choice. The difference is not absolute, and speakers may use different forms depending on what they want to emphasize:

> Jag ska ringa läkaren i morgon. <br />
> I'm going to call the doctor tomorrow. *(an intention)*

> Jag kommer att ringa läkaren i morgon. <br />
> I will call the doctor tomorrow. *(a statement about what is expected to happen)*

> Jag ringer läkaren i morgon. <br />
> I'm calling the doctor tomorrow. *(an arrangement or settled plan)*

Use the time expression and the surrounding conversation to make the intended time clear. Common expressions include **i morgon** (tomorrow), **i kväll** (this evening), **nästa vecka** (next week), **på fredag** (on Friday), and **om två dagar** (in two days).
