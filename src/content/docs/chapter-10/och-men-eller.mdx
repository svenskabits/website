**Och**, **men**, and **eller** are conjunctions: they connect words, phrases, or clauses. They connect parts of the same kind, and each conjunction shows a different relationship between those parts.

## **Och** adds information

Use **och** to add one thing to another. It means **and**. It can connect nouns, descriptions, actions, or complete clauses:

| What it connects | Swedish | English |
|---|---|---|
| Nouns | Vi köpte äpplen och päron. | We bought apples and pears. |
| Descriptions | Lägenheten är liten och ljus. | The apartment is small and bright. |
| Actions with the same subject | Hon kom hem och lagade middag. | She came home and made dinner. |
| Complete clauses | Jag lagar mat och min partner dukar. | I am cooking, and my partner is setting the table. |

When two actions have the same subject, the subject does not need to be repeated: **Hon kom hem och lagade middag.** If the next part has a different subject, include it: **Jag lagar mat och min partner dukar.**

**Och** can also connect events in a sequence. The order in which they are mentioned often suggests the order in which they happen:

> Först handlade vi och sedan gick vi hem.  
> First we did the shopping, and then we went home.

## **Men** shows a contrast

Use **men** to connect contrasting or unexpected information. It means **but**:

> Jag är trött, men jag vill följa med.  
> I am tired, but I want to come along.

> Bussen var sen, men vi kom fram i tid.  
> The bus was late, but we arrived on time.

> Hon tycker om kaffe, men inte te.  
> She likes coffee, but not tea.

For a direct correction meaning **not X but Y**, Swedish often uses **inte ... utan** rather than **inte ... men**:

> Jag tar inte bussen, utan tåget.  
> I am not taking the bus, but the train.

> Det är inte dyrt, utan ganska billigt.  
> It is not expensive, but quite cheap.

Use **men** when the second part contrasts with the first; use **utan** after **inte** when replacing or correcting the first choice.

## **Eller** gives alternatives

Use **eller** to present alternatives. It means **or**:

> Vill du ha kaffe eller te?  
> Would you like coffee or tea?

> Vi kan åka i dag eller i morgon.  
> We can go today or tomorrow.

> Ska vi gå på bio eller stanna hemma?  
> Should we go to the cinema or stay at home?

In questions, **eller** commonly introduces a choice. It can connect individual words or longer options, such as two actions. The listener can usually choose one, but the exact meaning depends on the situation.

The phrase **eller hur?** is commonly added to a statement when the speaker expects agreement. It is similar to **right?** or **isn't that so?**:

> Du bor nära stationen, eller hur?  
> You live near the station, right?

## Paired forms

Some useful expressions use two conjunctions together:

| Swedish pattern | Use | Example | English |
|---|---|---|---|
| **både ... och** | Includes two things: **both ... and** | Jag talar både svenska och engelska. | I speak both Swedish and English. |
| **antingen ... eller** | Presents a choice: **either ... or** | Vi kan antingen äta hemma eller gå ut. | We can either eat at home or go out. |
| **varken ... eller** | Excludes both: **neither ... nor** | Jag dricker varken kaffe eller te. | I drink neither coffee nor tea. |

With **varken ... eller**, do not normally add **inte** for the same negation. The phrase itself makes the sentence negative:

> Han har varken tid eller pengar.  
> He has neither time nor money.

## Word order and punctuation

When **och**, **men**, or **eller** connects two main clauses, each clause keeps its ordinary word order. In a statement, the verb follows the subject unless another sentence element comes first:

> Sara arbetar hemma, men hennes kollega arbetar på kontoret.  
> Sara works from home, but her colleague works at the office.

> På lördag åker vi till Uppsala och på söndag stannar vi hemma.  
> On Saturday we are going to Uppsala, and on Sunday we are staying at home.

In the second example, **på lördag** and **på söndag** come first in their respective clauses, so the verb comes before the subject in each one. The conjunction does not change this word order.

A comma is often used between two longer clauses, especially before **men**, to make the sentence easier to read. A comma is not usually needed when the conjunction connects only a few words or short phrases:

> Jag köpte bröd och mjölk.  
> I bought bread and milk.

> Jag ville ringa dig, men jag glömde det.  
> I wanted to call you, but I forgot.
