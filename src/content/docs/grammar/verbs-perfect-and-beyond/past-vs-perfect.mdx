---
title: Past vs. Perfect
description: Learn when Swedish uses preteritum or the perfect to talk about past events.
---

Swedish uses both **preteritum** (the past form) and the **perfect** (**har + supine**) for events that happened in the past. The choice depends on how you present the event: as part of a finished past time, or as an experience or event connected to the present. Context matters, and both forms can describe the same event from different perspectives.

## Choosing between the forms

Use **preteritum** when you place an event in a finished past time. Words such as **igår** (“yesterday”), **i lördags** (“last Saturday”), and **förra året** (“last year”) commonly go with preteritum:

- **Jag läste boken i helgen.** — I read the book this weekend.
- **Hon kom hem klockan sex.** — She came home at six.

Use the **perfect**, formed with **har + supine**, when the past event is presented as an experience or as relevant to the situation now. The focus is not on a finished past time:

- **Jag har läst boken.** — I’ve read the book.
- **Hon har kommit hem.** — She has come home.

These pairs can refer to the same reading or arrival. **Jag läste boken i helgen** says when it happened; **Jag har läst boken** presents the completed reading without saying when. **Hon kom hem klockan sex** gives the time; **Hon har kommit hem** can report that she is home now. The surrounding conversation helps determine which perspective fits.

## Finished past time and English

Swedish generally uses preteritum with an explicit finished-time expression. For example, say **Jag såg den igår** (“I saw it yesterday”), not the English-style **Jag har sett den igår**. In English, “I have seen it yesterday” is usually not idiomatic either; English normally uses “I saw it yesterday.” Without a stated finished time, Swedish perfect may describe experience, as in **Jag har sett den filmen** (“I’ve seen that film”).

This is a useful tendency, not a rule that the perfect must always describe a lasting result. The speaker’s focus and the context matter.

## Common confusion: translating *have* literally

Do not choose the Swedish perfect just because an English sentence uses “have.” Match the Swedish time expression and meaning instead: **Jag träffade henne igår** — “I met her yesterday,” but **Jag har träffat henne** — “I’ve met her.” Also remember that **har** is followed by the supine: **har sett**, not **har såg**.

## Useful Expressions

1. **Jag jobbade hemma igår.** — I worked from home yesterday.
2. **Har du varit i Stockholm?** — Have you been to Stockholm?
3. **Vi såg den filmen förra veckan.** — We saw that film last week.
4. **Jag har redan ätit.** — I’ve already eaten.
5. **Han ringde mig i morse.** — He called me this morning.
6. **De har bott här i tre år.** — They’ve lived here for three years.
7. **Hon köpte en ny cykel i lördags.** — She bought a new bike last Saturday.
8. **Har tåget redan gått?** — Has the train left already?
9. **Jag tappade mina nycklar på väg hem.** — I lost my keys on the way home.
10. **Vi har inte pratat med henne än.** — We haven’t spoken to her yet.
