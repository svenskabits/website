A yes/no question asks whether something is true, so the answer can be **yes** or **no**. In a direct Swedish question, put the **finite verb**—the verb that carries the tense—before the subject:

**finite verb + subject + the rest?**

| Statement | Yes/no question | English |
|---|---|---|
| **Du bor här.** | **Bor du här?** | You live here. / Do you live here? |
| **Hon arbetar i dag.** | **Arbetar hon i dag?** | She is working today. / Is she working today? |
| **Tåget kommer snart.** | **Kommer tåget snart?** | The train is coming soon. / Is the train coming soon? |

## No English-style do-support

Swedish does not add an extra verb meaning *do* or *does* to make an ordinary question. Move the main finite verb before the subject:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Bor du i Uppsala?** | Do you live in Uppsala? |
| **Har de tid?** | Do they have time? |
| **Gör hon kaffe?** | Is she making coffee? |

In **Gör hon kaffe?** (Is she making coffee?), **gör** is the main verb meaning *make/do*; it is not an extra question word.

## With modal and auxiliary verbs

If a sentence already has a modal or auxiliary verb, put that finite verb before the subject. The other verb stays after it:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Kan du simma?** | Can you swim? |
| **Vill ni äta nu?** | Do you want to eat now? |
| **Måste han gå?** | Does he have to go? |
| **Har du sett filmen?** | Have you seen the film? |

Only the finite verb moves before the subject. For example, in **Kan du hjälpa mig?** (Can you help me?), **kan** is first, then the subject **du**, then the infinitive **hjälpa**.

## Negatives and short answers

In a negative yes/no question, **inte** usually comes after the subject:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Arbetar du inte i dag?** | Aren't you working today? |
| **Kan hon inte komma?** | Can't she come? |

You can answer with **ja** (yes) or **nej** (no) alone, or repeat the finite verb. In a short negative answer, **inte** comes after that verb:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Är du redo? – Ja, det är jag.** | Are you ready? – Yes, I am. |
| **Är du redo? – Nej, det är jag inte.** | Are you ready? – No, I'm not. |
| **Kan du hjälpa mig? – Ja, det kan jag.** | Can you help me? – Yes, I can. |

## Direct and indirect questions

The verb-before-subject pattern is for a direct question. An indirect yes/no question uses **om** (if/whether) and ordinary statement order inside the clause:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Bor hon här?** | Does she live here? |
| **Jag undrar om hon bor här.** | I wonder if she lives here. |
| **Vet du om hon bor här?** | Do you know if she lives here? |

In **Vet du** (Do you know?), the whole sentence is a direct question, so **vet** comes before **du**. Inside it, **om hon bor här** (if she lives here) is an indirect question, so the subject **hon** comes before the verb **bor**. A direct question ends with a question mark; an indirect question inside a statement normally ends with a period.

Remember: for a direct yes/no question, put the finite verb before the subject. Do not add English-style *do* or *does*; use **om** for an indirect yes/no question.
