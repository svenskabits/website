Swedish has two common ways to talk about the past: **preteritum** (the simple past) and **perfect** (present-tense **har** + the supine). The choice is mainly about how you present the past event: as part of a finished past time, or as connected to the present. It is not a direct word-for-word match with English tense choices.

| Preteritum: finished past time | Perfect: connection to now or time not specified |
|---|---|
| **Jag såg den filmen i går.** — I saw that film yesterday. | **Jag har sett den filmen.** — I have seen that film. |
| **Vi bodde i Göteborg förra året.** — We lived in Gothenburg last year. | **Vi har bott i Göteborg i tre år.** — We have lived in Gothenburg for three years. |

## Use preteritum for a finished past time

Use preteritum when you say or clearly mean that something happened in a time that is over. The time can be stated directly or understood from the conversation.

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Vi reste till Malmö förra veckan.** | We travelled to Malmö last week. |
| **Hon ringde mig klockan sju.** | She called me at seven. |
| **De var i Spanien 2022.** | They were in Spain in 2022. |
| **Vad gjorde du i helgen?** | What did you do at the weekend? |

Typical time expressions include **i går** (yesterday), **förra veckan** (last week), **i somras** (last summer), and a finished year such as **2022**. These expressions do not mechanically require preteritum in every possible sentence, but they commonly locate an event in a completed past period.

Preteritum also describes past states, habits, and events in a story. It does not by itself mean that an action was completed in one moment:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **När jag var barn spelade jag fotboll varje dag.** | When I was a child, I played football every day. |
| **Det regnade hela natten.** | It rained all night. |
| **Jag läste när du ringde.** | I was reading when you called. |

## Use perfect when the past connects to now

Use the perfect when the exact past time is not important, when you talk about experience up to now, when a past event has a result that matters now, or when a period continues into the present.

| Use | Swedish | English |
|---|---|---|
| Experience; no finished time given | **Har du varit i Kiruna?** | Have you been to Kiruna? |
| Result that matters now | **Jag har tappat nyckeln.** | I have lost the key. |
| An open period, such as this year | **Det har regnat mycket i år.** | It has rained a lot this year. |
| A situation that began earlier and continues | **Hon har bott här i fem år.** | She has lived here for five years. |

With a situation that continues, **sedan** means “since” and **i** can mean “for” with a length of time:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Jag har arbetat här sedan 2021.** | I have worked here since 2021. |
| **Vi har varit här i två dagar.** | We have been here for two days. |

The perfect often occurs with **redan** (already), **än** (yet), or **aldrig** (never) when the speaker is talking about what has or has not happened up to now:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Har tåget redan kommit?** | Has the train arrived already? |
| **Jag har inte packat än.** | I have not packed yet. |
| **Vi har aldrig åkt skidor.** | We have never gone skiing. |

## The time expression is a clue, not the whole rule

An expression for a period that is still open, such as **i dag** (today) or **i år** (this year), often goes with the perfect when the speaker views the period as continuing:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Jag har träffat Anna i dag.** | I have met Anna today. |
| **Vi har haft en varm sommar i år.** | We have had a warm summer this year. |

But the expression alone does not choose the tense. A speaker can use preteritum with **i dag** if the event is presented as a completed event at a definite time:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Jag träffade Anna i dag klockan tio.** | I met Anna today at ten. |

The important question is how the speaker frames the event: as belonging to a completed past occasion, or as relevant to the present/open period. Context and the speaker's point of view matter, so avoid treating time words as rigid triggers.

## The same event can be described in either tense

Both tenses can be correct when the speaker chooses a different focus. Compare:

| Swedish | English | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| **Vi åt lunch klockan tolv.** | We ate lunch at twelve. | A completed event at a stated past time. |
| **Vi har redan ätit lunch.** | We have already eaten lunch. | The meal is done; that matters now, for example when deciding whether to order food. |
| **Har du sett den nya filmen?** | Have you seen the new film? | Whether you have the experience now; no time is given. |
| **Såg du den nya filmen i går?** | Did you see the new film yesterday? | Whether the event happened at a particular finished time. |

English often uses the simple past where Swedish uses the perfect, especially when asking about experience or reporting news without naming a finished time. Translate the meaning and time focus, rather than copying the English tense mechanically.

## Word order reminder

The tense choice does not change the basic placement of the verb. In an ordinary statement, the preterite verb follows the subject. In the perfect, **har** is the finite verb and the supine follows it:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Jag reste till Umeå i måndags.** | I travelled to Umeå on Monday. |
| **Jag har rest till Umeå flera gånger.** | I have travelled to Umeå several times. |

In a yes-or-no question, put the finite verb before the subject. With **inte**, put it after the finite verb:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Bodde ni i tält förra sommaren?** | Did you live in a tent last summer? |
| **Har ni bott på det här hotellet förut?** | Have you stayed at this hotel before? |
| **Vi åkte inte till stranden i går.** | We did not go to the beach yesterday. |
| **Vi har inte åkt till stranden än.** | We have not gone to the beach yet. |

Choose the tense first by deciding whether you are placing the event in a finished past time or connecting it to now. Then use the appropriate form: preteritum on its own, or **har** plus the supine for the perfect.
