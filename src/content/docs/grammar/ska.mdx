## Meaning and use

**Ska** is a modal verb. It is often used to talk about plans, intentions, and things someone expects or is supposed to do. The form is the same with every subject:

| Subject | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| jag, du, han/hon, vi, ni, de | **ska** | Vi **ska** äta middag hemma. — We are going to have dinner at home. |

The time is usually clear from the context or a time expression. Swedish often uses the present form **ska** for a future plan.

> Jag ska träffa en vän efter jobbet i morgon.  
> I'm going to meet a friend after work tomorrow.

> De ska flytta till Uppsala nästa månad.  
> They are going to move to Uppsala next month.

**Ska** can also express a decision, promise, or intention made by the speaker.

> Jag ska ringa dig i kväll.  
> I'll call you tonight.

It can describe what someone is expected, instructed, or required to do. In this use, the strength depends on the situation and tone; **ska** can sound like an instruction or rule.

> Alla ska komma i tid.  
> Everyone is supposed to arrive on time.

> Du ska ta den här medicinen efter maten.  
> You should take this medicine after the meal.

In a question, **ska** often asks about a plan, suggests doing something together, or offers help.

> Ska du jobba i helgen?  
> Are you going to work this weekend?

> Ska vi ta bussen?  
> Shall we take the bus?

> Ska jag öppna fönstret?  
> Shall I open the window?

## Form: ska + infinitive

After **ska**, use the infinitive of the next verb without **att** (the word “to”). The infinitive is the dictionary form, such as **äta**, **bo**, or **läsa**.

> Hon ska läsa en bok.  
> She is going to read a book.

> Vi ska inte köpa bil nu.  
> We are not going to buy a car now.

If another modal verb follows, it also stays in the infinitive, and the next verb follows it without **att**.

> Jag ska kunna svara på frågan i morgon.  
> I will be able to answer the question tomorrow.

## Word order

In a statement, put **ska** after the subject and before the infinitive:

> De ska resa på fredag.  
> They are going to travel on Friday.

Put **inte** after **ska** and before the infinitive:

> De ska inte resa på fredag.  
> They are not going to travel on Friday.

For a yes/no question, put **ska** before the subject:

> Ska de resa på fredag?  
> Are they going to travel on Friday?

With a question word, put the question word first, then **ska**, then the subject:

> När ska de resa?  
> When are they going to travel?

In a subordinate clause after words such as **att** (“that”), **om** (“if/whether”), or **eftersom** (“because”), **inte** usually comes before **ska**:

> Jag tror att de inte ska resa på fredag.  
> I think that they are not going to travel on Friday.

## Ska and other ways to talk about the future

Use **ska** especially when a person has a plan or intention, or when someone is giving an instruction. **Kommer att** often presents a prediction or expectation, rather than a person's plan. The difference is not absolute, and context matters.

> Jag ska börja en kurs i höst.  
> I'm going to start a course this autumn. *(a plan)*

> Det kommer att regna i kväll.  
> It will rain this evening. *(a prediction)*

The present tense alone can also refer to an arranged future event when a time is given.

> Tåget går klockan åtta i morgon.  
> The train leaves at eight tomorrow.

## The form skulle

**Skulle** is the past form of **ska**, but it is also common in polite requests and hypothetical situations. With an infinitive, it follows the same pattern as **ska**: there is no **att** before the next verb.

It can report a plan or intention from the past, often one that did not happen:

> Vi skulle äta ute, men restaurangen var stängd.  
> We were going to eat out, but the restaurant was closed.

It can also make a request sound more polite:

> Skulle du kunna hjälpa mig?  
> Could you help me?

**Skall** is an alternative spelling of **ska**. **Ska** is the usual spelling in modern everyday Swedish; **skall** can appear in more formal or traditional writing.
