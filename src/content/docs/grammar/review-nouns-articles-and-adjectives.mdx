Swedish nouns have a grammatical group, singular and plural forms, and definite and indefinite forms. Articles and adjectives change according to these forms. The key is to identify the noun first, then make the other words agree with it.

## Noun groups and forms

Every Swedish noun is an **en-word** or an **ett-word**. The group is grammatical, not a description of the thing's natural sex, and usually has to be learned with the noun. Many noun endings give clues, but none predicts every word.

| Noun group | Indefinite singular | Definite singular | Indefinite plural | Definite plural |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| En-word | **en bil** — a car | **bilen** — the car | **bilar** — cars | **bilarna** — the cars |
| Ett-word | **ett hus** — a house | **huset** — the house | **hus** — houses | **husen** — the houses |

The definite singular is usually made by adding an ending to the noun. En-words commonly take **-en** after a consonant or **-n** after a vowel; ett-words commonly take **-et** after a consonant or **-t** after a vowel. The exact plural ending depends on the noun's plural pattern:

| Indefinite singular | Definite singular | Indefinite plural | Definite plural |
|---|---|---|---|
| **en flicka** — a girl | **flickan** — the girl | **flickor** — girls | **flickorna** — the girls |
| **ett äpple** — an apple | **äpplet** — the apple | **äpplen** — apples | **äpplena** — the apples |
| **en bok** — a book | **boken** — the book | **böcker** — books | **böckerna** — the books |

Many plurals end in **-or**, **-ar**, **-er**, or **-n**; some have no added ending, and some change their stem or vowel. These patterns are useful, but not fully predictable, so learn the plural and definite forms with the noun. In compounds, the final part usually determines the noun group, and endings attach to the whole compound: **ett sovrum** (a bedroom), **sovrummet** (the bedroom), **sovrum** (bedrooms).

## Choosing an article—or no article

Use **en** or **ett** with a singular countable noun when introducing one unspecified example. Use the definite noun form when the listener can identify the thing, for example because it has already been mentioned or is clear from the situation:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Jag såg en hund. Hunden var blöt.** | I saw a dog. The dog was wet. |
| **Kan du stänga fönstret?** | Can you close the window? |

An ordinary indefinite plural has no article. Use the definite plural when referring to an identifiable group:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Vi behöver stolar.** | We need chairs. |
| **Stolarna vid fönstret är lediga.** | The chairs by the window are free. |

Swedish also often uses no article with substances, abstract ideas, and activities in a general sense, and with many professions after **vara** (to be):

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Jag dricker kaffe varje morgon.** | I drink coffee every morning. |
| **Musik gör henne glad.** | Music makes her happy. |
| **Han är läkare.** | He is a doctor. |

English and Swedish do not always use articles in the same places. Decide whether the Swedish noun is a specific countable thing, an unspecified one, or a general substance or idea rather than translating each English **a** or **the** mechanically.

## Adjective agreement

An adjective changes to match the noun it describes. The usual forms for **stor** (big) are:

| Noun | Adjective form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Singular en-word | **stor** | **en stor bil** — a big car |
| Singular ett-word | **stort** | **ett stort hus** — a big house |
| Plural | **stora** | **stora bilar** — big cars |

This agreement applies whether the adjective is before an indefinite noun or follows a linking verb such as **är** (is/are):

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Bilen är stor.** | The car is big. |
| **Huset är stort.** | The house is big. |
| **Bilarna är stora.** | The cars are big. |

The subject can be definite, but an adjective after **är** still uses the en-word, ett-word, or plural form. It does not take the definite adjective form just because the noun means *the*.

Many adjectives follow the usual pattern, but spelling can change when an ending is added, and some forms are irregular or unchanged. For example, **röd, rött, röda** (red) changes its spelling; **liten, litet, små** (small) has irregular plural **små**; and **bra** (good) stays the same:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **ett rött äpple** | a red apple |
| **små barn** | small children |
| **Böckerna är bra.** | The books are good. |

## Adjectives with definite nouns

When an adjective comes before an ordinary definite noun, Swedish normally uses a separate definite word before the adjective as well as the definite ending on the noun. This is often called **double definiteness**:

- **den** + adjective + definite en-word
- **det** + adjective + definite ett-word
- **de** + adjective + definite plural

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **den stora bilen** | the big car |
| **det stora huset** | the big house |
| **de stora bilarna** | the big cars |

The adjective in this construction usually ends in **-a**, though its form may be irregular: **den lilla katten** (the small cat), **de små katterna** (the small cats). For male people, **-e** is also possible, alongside **-a**: **den unge mannen** or **den unga mannen** (the young man).

With a possessive, there is normally no **den/det/de** and no definite ending on the noun. The adjective usually has its definite form:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **min nya bil** | my new car |
| **mitt nya hus** | my new house |
| **mina nya bilar** | my new cars |
| **Annas röda jacka** | Anna's red jacket |

Demonstratives such as **den här** (this) and **de där** (those) take a definite noun. When an adjective is included, the noun and adjective both appear in the definite phrase:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **den här bilen** | this car |
| **den här stora bilen** | this big car |
| **de där gamla husen** | those old houses |

## More than one noun or adjective

An adjective agrees with the noun it describes, not with the person speaking. If a noun phrase has several adjectives, each normally has the appropriate form:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **ett ljust, modernt kök** | a bright, modern kitchen |
| **de ljusa, moderna köken** | the bright, modern kitchens |

When one adjective describes a group of different nouns, the adjective is normally plural:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Bilen och huset är gamla.** | The car and the house are old. |

Adjectives can also be compared. Regular forms often use **-are** and **-ast**, while some use **mer** (more) and **mest** (most). Common irregular forms include **bra, bättre, bäst** (good, better, best):

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Den här vägen är kortare.** | This route is shorter. |
| **Det här är den mest intressanta boken.** | This is the most interesting book. |
| **Det var den bästa lösningen.** | It was the best solution. |

## Possession and noun phrases

Add **-s** to a name or noun to show possession. The possessor comes before the thing owned, and the possessed noun does not take a definite ending after the possessive:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Annas cykel** | Anna's bicycle |
| **min brors lägenhet** | my brother's apartment |
| **stadens gamla bibliotek** | the city's old library |

When a possessive word comes before a noun, use its ordinary noun form: **min bil** (my car), not *min bilen*. Similarly, use **mina böcker** (my books), not *mina böckerna*. A possessive already identifies the noun.

## A quick check

When checking a noun phrase, work from the noun outward:

1. Is it an **en-word** or **ett-word**? Is it singular or plural?
2. Is the noun indefinite or definite? Does it need an article, a definite ending, or no article?
3. Does an adjective agree with the noun? If the phrase is definite, does it need **den/det/de** and a definite adjective form?
4. Is a possessive or demonstrative present? Check that the noun has the right form and does not get an extra definite ending.

For example, **ett hus → huset → det stora huset**, but **mitt hus → mitt stora hus**: the noun's group determines the form, and the words around it show whether it is definite.
