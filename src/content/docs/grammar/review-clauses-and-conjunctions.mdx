A **clause** is a group of words built around a verb. A sentence can contain one clause or several. Conjunctions connect words, phrases, or clauses, and the kind of conjunction you choose affects both the meaning and, sometimes, the word order.

## Main clauses and subordinate clauses

A **main clause** can usually stand on its own as a sentence. In a statement, its finite verb—the verb that carries tense, or the first verb in a verb phrase—normally comes in the second clause position. This is the V2 pattern:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Vi stannar hemma i kväll.** | We are staying at home tonight. |
| **I kväll stannar vi hemma.** | Tonight we are staying at home. |
| **Vi har inte bestämt oss än.** | We have not decided yet. |

A **subordinate clause** depends on a main clause or another clause. It often begins with a subordinating conjunction such as **att** (that), **eftersom** (because), or **om** (if/whether). It usually cannot stand alone in the same way. Its usual order is:

> **subordinating word + subject + sentence adverb + finite verb + rest**

In a subordinate clause, sentence adverbs such as **inte** (not), **aldrig** (never), and **ofta** (often) normally come before the finite verb. Compare:

| Main clause | Subordinate clause |
|---|---|
| **Hon har inte tid.** — She does not have time. | **Jag vet att hon inte har tid.** — I know that she does not have time. |
| **De kommer aldrig sent.** — They are never late. | **Vi märkte att de aldrig kom sent.** — We noticed that they were never late. |

In the main clause, **har** and **kommer** come before **inte** and **aldrig**. In the subordinate clauses, the adverbs come before the finite verbs **har** and **kom**.

## Coordinating conjunctions: joining equals

Coordinating conjunctions join elements of the same kind, such as two nouns, two phrases, or two main clauses. When they join main clauses, each clause keeps main-clause word order; a conjunction does not make the next clause subordinate.

| Conjunction | Common meaning | Swedish | English |
|---|---|---|---|
| **och** | and | **Hon läste rapporten och skrev ett mejl.** | She read the report and wrote an email. |
| **men** | but | **Jag ville följa med, men jag var sjuk.** | I wanted to come along, but I was ill. |
| **eller** | or | **Ska vi ta bussen eller går vi?** | Shall we take the bus or walk? |
| **för** | for, because | **Vi tog en taxi, för det regnade.** | We took a taxi, because it was raining. |
| **så** | so, and as a result | **Det blev sent, så vi gick hem.** | It got late, so we went home. |

The conjunction **för** gives a reason and **så** introduces a result. Both connect main clauses, so the second clause has main-clause word order: **det regnade**, **vi gick**. In everyday speech, **eftersom** is often a more usual way to introduce a reason; it makes a subordinate clause: **Vi tog en taxi eftersom det regnade.** (We took a taxi because it was raining.)

If two coordinated clauses have the same subject, the subject may be stated just once:

> **Jag gick hem och lagade middag.** — I went home and made dinner.

Here **jag** is the subject of both verbs. If each clause has a different subject, state each one: **Jag gick hem och min sambo lagade middag.** (I went home and my partner cooked dinner.)

## Subordinating conjunctions and the clauses they introduce

Subordinating conjunctions introduce a dependent clause. The conjunction's meaning helps show how that clause relates to the main clause.

### That and indirect questions

Use **att** to introduce a statement that is part of another clause. In this use, **att** means “that.” It is often left out in informal Swedish when the meaning is clear, but it is common and natural to include it:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Hon säger att tåget är försenat.** | She says that the train is delayed. |
| **Jag tror (att) vi hinner.** | I think we will make it in time. |

An indirect question is also subordinate and uses subordinate word order. Use **om** for an embedded yes-or-no question, or keep the question word for other questions:

| Direct question | Indirect question |
|---|---|
| **Kommer hon i kväll?** — Is she coming tonight? | **Jag undrar om hon kommer i kväll.** — I wonder whether she is coming tonight. |
| **Var bor de?** — Where do they live? | **Vet du var de bor?** — Do you know where they live? |
| **Varför gick han tidigt?** — Why did he leave early? | **Hon frågade varför han gick tidigt.** — She asked why he left early. |

Do not use direct-question word order inside an indirect question: **var de bor**, not **var bor de**.

### Reasons and causes

Use **eftersom** or **därför att** to give a reason. The clause after either expression is subordinate:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Vi stannade inne eftersom det blåste mycket.** | We stayed inside because it was very windy. |
| **Hon tog ledigt därför att hennes barn var sjukt.** | She took time off because her child was ill. |

The conjunction can also begin the sentence. The subordinate clause keeps its own word order; the following main clause follows V2, so the finite verb comes before its subject:

> **Eftersom det blåste mycket stannade vi inne.** — Because it was very windy, we stayed inside.

### Time

Time conjunctions locate one event in relation to another. Common ones include **när** (when), **medan** (while), **innan** (before), **efter att** (after), and **tills** (until):

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Ring mig när du kommer hem.** | Call me when you get home. |
| **Hon lyssnade på musik medan hon lagade mat.** | She listened to music while she cooked. |
| **Tvätta händerna innan du äter.** | Wash your hands before you eat. |
| **Efter att vi hade ätit gick vi en promenad.** | After we had eaten, we went for a walk. |
| **Vi väntade tills bussen kom.** | We waited until the bus came. |

With **efter att**, the word **att** belongs to the conjunction. The event that happened earlier can be in the pluperfect (**hade** + supine), as in **hade ätit**, but the preterite is also possible when the order is already clear: **Efter att vi åt gick vi en promenad.** (After we ate, we went for a walk.)

### Conditions

Use **om** to introduce a condition (“if”). Use **ifall** or **såvida** in some of the same contexts; **såvida** is more formal and is often used with **inte**:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Om det blir soligt, äter vi lunch ute.** | If it is sunny, we will eat lunch outside. |
| **Ta med en jacka ifall det blir kallt.** | Bring a jacket in case it gets cold. |
| **Du får låna bilen såvida du inte kör för långt.** | You may borrow the car as long as you do not drive too far. |

When an **om** clause comes first, it is followed by a main clause with V2 order: **Om det blir soligt, äter vi ute**—not *om det blir soligt, vi äter ute*.

### Contrast and concession

Use **fast**, **fastän**, **trots att**, or **även om** to introduce a contrast or something that might have been expected to prevent the main-clause event. They introduce subordinate clauses:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Vi gick en promenad fast det regnade.** | We went for a walk although it was raining. |
| **Trots att hon var trött fortsatte hon arbeta.** | Although she was tired, she continued working. |
| **Även om det blir dyrt vill de genomföra planen.** | Even if it becomes expensive, they want to carry out the plan. |

**Fast** is common in speech and everyday writing. **Även om** can refer to a real possibility (“even if”) or something generally true (“even though”), depending on context.

### Purpose and result

Use **så att** before a finite clause to express a purpose or an intended result:

> **Jag skriver ner adressen så att jag inte glömmer den.** — I am writing down the address so that I do not forget it.

Use **för att** before an infinitive phrase when the understood subject of the infinitive is usually the same as the subject of the main clause:

> **Hon tog bussen för att komma i tid.** — She took the bus in order to arrive on time.

The structure **så … att** can also express degree and consequence, often with a word such as **så** (so) or **så mycket** (so much):

> **Det regnade så mycket att matchen ställdes in.** — It rained so much that the match was cancelled.

In this result pattern, **att** introduces a finite clause; it is not the infinitive marker.

## Relative clauses

A relative clause describes a person or thing mentioned in the main clause. It often begins with **som**, which can mean “who,” “which,” or “that.” It uses subordinate word order:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Jag har en kollega som arbetar hemifrån.** | I have a colleague who works from home. |
| **Det här är boken som jag köpte i går.** | This is the book that I bought yesterday. |
| **Vi bor i ett hus som inte har någon hiss.** | We live in a house that does not have an elevator. |

In the first sentence, **som** is the subject of **arbetar**. In the second, **jag** is the subject and **som** refers to the object **boken**. In the third, **inte** comes before the finite verb **har**, as it normally does in a subordinate clause.

## Do not confuse conjunctions with linking adverbs

Some words have similar meanings but connect clauses differently. **Men** and **så** are coordinating conjunctions: the clause after them keeps main-clause word order. **Därför** (therefore) is an adverb; when it begins a main clause, the finite verb still comes second, before the subject:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Det snöade, så vi tog bilen.** | It was snowing, so we took the car. |
| **Det snöade. Därför tog vi bilen.** | It was snowing. Therefore, we took the car. |
| **Jag var trött, men jag fortsatte arbeta.** | I was tired, but I continued working. |
| **Jag var trött. Ändå fortsatte jag arbeta.** | I was tired. Still, I continued working. |

After **så**, the subject comes before the verb (**vi tog**). After sentence-initial **därför** or **ändå**, the finite verb comes before the subject (**tog vi**, **fortsatte jag**) because of V2.

## A quick way to choose

When connecting clauses, ask:

1. Are the clauses equal, often two main clauses? Use a coordinating conjunction such as **och**, **men**, **eller**, **för**, or **så**. Keep main-clause word order in both.
2. Does one clause explain, limit, or depend on the other? Choose a subordinating conjunction such as **att**, **eftersom**, **om**, **när**, or **trots att**. Put sentence adverbs such as **inte** before the finite verb in that clause.
3. Does the second clause give a result but start with an adverb such as **därför**? Treat it as a new main clause and keep V2 order.
4. Does a subordinate clause come first? Keep subordinate word order inside it, then use V2 in the following main clause.

> **Även om jag inte har mycket tid försöker jag läsa svenska varje dag.** — Even though I do not have much time, I try to read Swedish every day.

The clause introduced by **även om** has subordinate order (**jag inte har**). The following main clause follows V2 (**försöker jag**) because the subordinate clause is first.
