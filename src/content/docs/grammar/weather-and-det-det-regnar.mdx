Swedish often uses **det** as the subject in sentences about the weather. In **Det regnar.** (It is raining.), **det** does not mean a particular “it.” It is a grammatical subject, like the placeholder **it** in English weather sentences.

## Weather verbs with det

Use **det** before weather verbs such as **regna** (rain), **snöa** (snow), **blåsa** (blow), **hagla** (hail), and **åska** (thunder). The verb form does not change to match the amount of rain, snow, or wind.

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Det regnar mycket i dag.** | It is raining a lot today. |
| **Det snöar i fjällen.** | It is snowing in the mountains. |
| **Det blåser vid kusten.** | It is windy on the coast. |
| **Det haglar.** | It is hailing. |
| **Det åskar i kväll.** | There is a thunderstorm this evening. |

Some weather descriptions use **vara** (be) and an adjective instead:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Det är kallt ute.** | It is cold outside. |
| **Det är soligt och varmt.** | It is sunny and warm. |
| **Det är molnigt i dag.** | It is cloudy today. |
| **Det är blåsigt vid havet.** | It is windy by the sea. |

With **det är**, adjectives describing the general weather usually take the neuter **-t** form: **kallt**, **soligt**, **molnigt**, **blåsigt**. Compare this with **en solig dag** (a sunny day), where **solig** is the form used with an *en*-word such as **dag**.

You can also describe changing weather with **bli** (become): **Det blir kallare i natt.** (It will get colder tonight.) **Det blev soligt efter lunch.** (It became sunny after lunch.)

## Talking about weather at different times

Weather verbs change tense like other Swedish verbs. The subject **det** stays in place:

| Present | Past | Supine | English |
|---|---|---|---|
| **regnar** | **regnade** | **regnat** | rain |
| **snöar** | **snöade** | **snöat** | snow |
| **blåser** | **blåste** | **blåst** | blow |

Use the past form for a finished event in the past. Use **har** with the supine to say that it has happened or continued up to now:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Det regnade hela natten.** | It rained all night. |
| **Det snöade när vi kom hem.** | It was snowing when we got home. |
| **Det har blåst mycket i veckan.** | It has been very windy this week. |
| **Det har regnat sedan i morse.** | It has been raining since this morning. |

For a forecast or expectation, use **ska** or **kommer att** with the infinitive:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Det ska regna i morgon.** | It is going to rain tomorrow. |
| **Det kommer att snöa i kväll.** | It will snow this evening. |
| **Det ska bli varmt i helgen.** | It is going to be warm this weekend. |

## Word order and questions

In a simple statement, **det** comes before the verb: **Det regnar nu.** (It is raining now.) If another part of the sentence comes first, the verb comes before **det**:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **I dag regnar det.** | Today it is raining. |
| **På vintern snöar det ofta här.** | In winter it often snows here. |
| **Därför stannar vi hemma när det blåser.** | That is why we stay home when it is windy. |

This is the usual Swedish main-clause word order: the verb stays in second position. In the last example, **när det blåser** is a subordinate clause, so **det** comes before the verb there.

For a yes-or-no question, put the verb before **det**. With a question word, put the question word first:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Regnar det fortfarande?** | Is it still raining? |
| **Blåste det mycket i går?** | Was it very windy yesterday? |
| **Varför snöar det så mycket?** | Why is it snowing so much? |

## When det is not the weather subject

Not every Swedish weather sentence uses **det**. A real thing can be the subject when it is the thing doing the action:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Solen skiner.** | The sun is shining. |
| **Vinden blåser från norr.** | The wind is blowing from the north. |
| **Regnet har slutat.** | The rain has stopped. |

Compare **Solen skiner.** (The sun is shining.) with **Det är soligt.** (It is sunny.) In the first sentence, **solen** is the subject. In the second, **det** is the general, impersonal subject used to describe the weather.
