Every Swedish noun belongs to one of two grammatical groups: **en-words** and **ett-words**. The group is part of the noun, so learn the article with each new word: **en stol** (a chair), **ett bord** (a table).

In grammar books, en-words are often called *common-gender nouns* and ett-words *neuter nouns*. These are grammatical labels, not descriptions of a person or object's sex. Most Swedish nouns are en-words, but there is no reliable rule for predicting every noun's group.

## Choosing en or ett

Use **en** or **ett** before a singular countable noun when you mean one, unspecified thing. English uses *a* or *an* for both.

| Group | Swedish | English |
|---|---|---|
| En-word | **en katt** | a cat |
| Ett-word | **ett äpple** | an apple |

The choice depends on the noun, not on the sound or first letter that follows:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| Hon har **en idé**. | She has an idea. |
| Han har **ett jobb**. | He has a job. |

**En** and **ett** can also be the number *one*. Stress the word when you want to emphasize the number: **Jag vill ha en, inte två.** (I want one, not two.)

## Learning a noun's group

Learn the article as part of the vocabulary item, for example **en nyckel** (a key) or **ett fönster** (a window). Some endings are useful clues, but they are tendencies rather than rules:

| Often en-words | Often ett-words |
|---|---|
| **-ning**: **en tidning** (a newspaper) | **-ande/-ende** for an action or result: **ett meddelande** (a message) |
| **-het**: **en möjlighet** (a possibility) | **-eri**: **ett bageri** (a bakery) |
| **-tion/-sion**: **en station** (a station) | **-um**: **ett museum** (a museum) |
| **-else**: **en händelse** (an event) | |

There are exceptions, and some endings occur in both groups. For instance, a word ending in **-ande** can name a person and be an en-word: **en sökande** (an applicant). A person's or animal's sex does not determine grammatical group: **ett barn** (a child) and **ett lejon** (a lion) are ett-words.

## Singular and plural forms

The noun's group is visible in some other words and forms, but plural endings must be learned with the noun. The examples below show common patterns, not a single rule for all nouns.

| Form | En-word: **bil** (car) | Ett-word: **hus** (house) |
|---|---|---|
| Indefinite singular | **en bil** — a car | **ett hus** — a house |
| Definite singular | **bilen** — the car | **huset** — the house |
| Indefinite plural | **bilar** — cars | **hus** — houses |
| Definite plural | **bilarna** — the cars | **husen** — the houses |

In the definite singular, the noun usually gets an ending rather than a separate word for *the*. The exact ending depends on the noun's form: **en flicka** becomes **flickan** (the girl), while **ett äpple** becomes **äpplet** (the apple). In the plural, the ending also varies: **en stol** becomes **stolar** (chairs), while **ett rum** becomes **rum** (rooms).

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| Jag köper **en stol**. | I am buying a chair. |
| **Stolen** är dyr. | The chair is expensive. |
| Vi köper **stolar**. | We are buying chairs. |
| **Stolarna** är dyra. | The chairs are expensive. |
| De bor i **ett hus**. | They live in a house. |
| **Huset** är gammalt. | The house is old. |
| Det finns **hus** här. | There are houses here. |
| **Husen** är gamla. | The houses are old. |

An ordinary indefinite plural has no **en** or **ett**. Use a plural form after a number: **två katter** (two cats), not *två en katt*.

## Adjectives agree with the noun

An adjective describing an indefinite singular noun usually has no extra ending with an en-word and takes **-t** with an ett-word:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **en stor bil** | a big car |
| **ett stort hus** | a big house |

The same agreement appears when the adjective follows a verb such as **är** (is):

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Bilen är stor.** | The car is big. |
| **Huset är stort.** | The house is big. |
| **Bilarna är stora.** | The cars are big. |
| **Husen är stora.** | The houses are big. |

In the plural, adjectives usually end in **-a** for both noun groups. Some common adjectives do not change: **en bra bok** (a good book), **ett bra hus** (a good house), **bra böcker** (good books). A few have less predictable forms, such as **en liten katt** (a small cat), **ett litet hus** (a small house), and **små katter** (small cats).

With an adjective before a definite noun, Swedish normally uses **den** with a singular en-word, **det** with a singular ett-word, and **de** with a plural noun. The adjective usually ends in **-a**, and the noun has its definite ending:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **den stora bilen** | the big car |
| **det stora huset** | the big house |
| **de stora bilarna** | the big cars |
| **de stora husen** | the big houses |

## Other words that match the noun

When **den** or **det** stands in for a noun, choose the form to match the noun's group. English usually uses *it* for either group.

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Bilen är röd. Den är ny.** | The car is red. It is new. |
| **Huset är rött. Det är nytt.** | The house is red. It is new. |

The forms also appear with **här** (here) and **där** (there): **den här bilen** (this car), **det här huset** (this house), **den där stolen** (that chair), **det där bordet** (that table). In the plural use **de här** or **de där** with either noun group: **de här bilarna** (these cars), **de där husen** (those houses).

Possessive words agree with the thing possessed for some owners. The same noun-group difference appears with **din/ditt** and **min/mitt**; plural nouns take **dina/mina**:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **min bok** | my book |
| **mitt rum** | my room |
| **mina böcker** | my books |
| **mina rum** | my rooms |
| **din jacka** | your jacket |
| **ditt namn** | your name |

Other possessives, such as **hans** (his), **hennes** (her), and **deras** (their), stay the same with all nouns: **hans bil** (his car), **hans hus** (his house). These forms identify the owner; they do not agree with the possessed noun.

The reflexive possessive **sin/sitt/sina** also agrees with the noun, but refers to the subject's own thing: **Han tar sin bok.** (He takes his own book.) **Han tar sitt kort.** (He takes his own card.) **De tar sina böcker.** (They take their own books.)

## Compounds

A compound is a word made by joining words together. Its group usually comes from its final part, which names the main thing:

| Main noun | Compound | English |
|---|---|---|
| **en nyckel** (a key) | **en bilnyckel** | a car key |
| **ett kort** (a card) | **ett bankkort** | a bank card |
| **ett rum** (a room) | **ett sovrum** | a bedroom |
| **en bok** (a book) | **en kokbok** | a cookbook |

The first part does not determine the group. For example, **ett bilsäte** (a car seat) is an ett-word because **säte** (seat) is the main noun. A compound is still a vocabulary item of its own, so check it if you are unsure.

## Nouns without en or ett

Do not put **en** or **ett** before an ordinary plural noun. Indefinite plural nouns appear without an article when you mean things in general or an unspecified number:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Katter tycker om värme.** | Cats like warmth. |
| **Vi behöver stolar.** | We need chairs. |
| **Jag har två katter.** | I have two cats. |

Swedish also often uses a noun without **en** or **ett** when it refers generally to a substance, idea, or activity rather than one countable item: **Kaffe är varmt.** (Coffee is hot.) **Hon tycker om musik.** (She likes music.) Whether a noun is countable and how it is used matter; do not add an article just because English has one.

## Common mistakes

| Avoid | Use | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *ett bil* | **en bil** (a car) | **Bil** is an en-word. |
| *en hus* | **ett hus** (a house) | **Hus** is an ett-word. |
| *en äpple* | **ett äpple** (an apple) | **Äpple** is an ett-word. |
| *ett stort bil* | **en stor bil** (a big car) | Both the article and adjective agree with the noun. |

The most useful habit is to learn the noun together with **en** or **ett**. The group affects the singular article, definite form, some adjective endings, and words such as **den/det** and **min/mitt**.
