Swedish word order depends first on the kind of clause you are writing. Main clauses follow the **V2 rule**: the finite verb—the verb that carries tense, or the first verb in a verb phrase—comes in the second clause position. Subordinate clauses use a different order, especially for the subject, sentence adverbs such as **inte** (not), and the finite verb.

## Main clauses: the finite verb is in second position

The first position can be the subject, a time or place phrase, an object, or even a whole subordinate clause. The finite verb still comes second. “Position” means a clause element, not necessarily one word.

| First element | Finite verb | Rest of the clause | English |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Jag** | **arbetar** | hemma i dag. | I am working at home today. |
| **I dag** | **arbetar** | jag hemma. | Today I am working at home. |
| **På biblioteket** | **träffade** | vi en gammal vän. | At the library we met an old friend. |
| **Den här boken** | **har** | jag redan läst. | I have already read this book. |

When something other than the subject is first, the subject normally comes after the finite verb. This is called **inversion**:

> **Efter jobbet ska jag handla mat.** — After work I am going to buy food.

The first element **Efter jobbet** is one time phrase; **ska** is the finite verb in second position, followed by the subject **jag**. Keep this order even when the first element is long:

> **Eftersom affären stänger tidigt i dag måste vi gå nu.** — Because the shop closes early today, we have to go now.

Here the whole subordinate clause comes first. The following main clause begins with **måste**, then the subject **vi**.

## Sentence adverbs and negation

In a main clause, sentence adverbs usually come after the finite verb. This includes **inte**, **aldrig** (never), **ofta** (often), **redan** (already), and **kanske** (perhaps). With a verb phrase, the adverb normally follows the first verb:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Jag kommer inte i morgon.** | I am not coming tomorrow. |
| **Hon har redan bokat biljetterna.** | She has already booked the tickets. |
| **Vi kan kanske ta tåget.** | Perhaps we can take the train. |

After a different first element, the finite verb remains second and the sentence adverb follows it:

> **I morgon kommer jag inte.** — Tomorrow I am not coming.

In a subordinate clause, these adverbs usually come before the finite verb. This difference is one of the clearest ways to tell the two clause patterns apart:

| Main clause | Subordinate clause |
|---|---|
| **Hon kommer inte i morgon.** — She is not coming tomorrow. | **Jag vet att hon inte kommer i morgon.** — I know that she is not coming tomorrow. |
| **De har aldrig träffat honom.** — They have never met him. | **… eftersom de aldrig har träffat honom.** — … because they have never met him. |

The adverb comes after the finite verb in the main clause (**har aldrig**) but before it in the subordinate clause (**aldrig har**). Other adverbials, such as time, place, and manner phrases, are more flexible and can be moved to the beginning for emphasis. The V2 rule still applies in a main clause.

## Subordinate clauses

A subordinate clause depends on another clause. It often begins with a word such as **att** (that), **eftersom** (because), **när** (when), or **om** (if/whether). Its usual order is:

> **subordinating word + subject + sentence adverb + finite verb + rest**

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Jag tror att hon kommer snart.** | I think that she will come soon. |
| **Vi stannade hemma eftersom det regnade.** | We stayed home because it was raining. |
| **Ring mig när du har tid.** | Call me when you have time. |
| **Han frågade om jag inte ville följa med.** | He asked whether I didn't want to come along. |

If the subordinate clause comes first, it still uses subordinate word order, and the following main clause still follows V2:

> **När mötet är slut går vi och äter lunch.** — When the meeting is over, we will go and have lunch.

The first clause has **subject + finite verb** (**mötet är**). In the main clause, **går** comes before the subject **vi** because the subordinate clause fills the first position.

Coordinating words such as **och** (and), **men** (but), and **eller** (or) can join main clauses. Each clause keeps main-clause word order:

> **Jag ringde henne, men hon svarade inte.** — I called her, but she did not answer.

By contrast, **eftersom** introduces a subordinate clause, where **inte** comes before the finite verb:

> **Jag ringde henne eftersom hon inte svarade.** — I called her because she did not answer.

## Questions

In a direct yes-or-no question, put the finite verb before the subject. Sentence adverbs usually follow the subject:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Kommer du i kväll?** | Are you coming tonight? |
| **Har hon inte läst meddelandet?** | Hasn't she read the message? |
| **Kan vi kanske börja nu?** | Can we perhaps start now? |

In a direct question with a question word, put the question word first, then the finite verb, then the subject:

> **Varför åkte ni så tidigt?** — Why did you leave so early?

An **indirect question** is part of another clause and uses subordinate word order. The question word remains at the beginning of the embedded question, but the subject comes before the finite verb. For an embedded yes-or-no question, use **om**:

| Direct question | Indirect question |
|---|---|
| **Var bor han?** — Where does he live? | **Vet du var han bor?** — Do you know where he lives? |
| **Har hon tid?** — Does she have time? | **Jag undrar om hon har tid.** — I wonder whether she has time. |

Do not carry the direct-question order into the embedded question: **var han bor** (where he lives), not **var bor han?** (Where does he live?).

## Relative clauses

A relative clause gives more information about a noun. It often begins with **som** (who, which, or that) and uses subordinate word order:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Jag har en granne som alltid hjälper mig.** | I have a neighbor who always helps me. |
| **Det är filmen som vi såg i går.** | It is the film that we saw yesterday. |

In the first example, **som** is the subject, followed by the sentence adverb **alltid** and the verb **hjälper**. In the second, **som** is the object of **såg**, so the subject **vi** comes before the verb.

## Objects, pronouns, and verb phrases

In a neutral statement, an object commonly follows the verb or verb phrase. A short object pronoun normally comes before longer time, place, or manner information:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Hon läste rapporten på tåget.** | She read the report on the train. |
| **Hon läste den på tåget.** | She read it on the train. |
| **Vi ska skicka dem i eftermiddag.** | We are going to send them this afternoon. |

With a verb phrase, the finite verb comes first in a main clause and sentence adverbs usually follow it. The remaining verbs stay together after the adverb, with objects after the verb phrase. For example, **har läst** means “have read,” and **ska läsa** means “will read” or “am going to read”:

> **Jag har inte kunnat hitta nycklarna.** — I have not been able to find the keys.

With a particle verb, keep the particle with the verb, before an object pronoun:

> **Kan du stänga av den?** — Can you turn it off?

## Imperatives

In a direct command or request, the imperative verb usually comes first. This is a command pattern, not the V2 pattern for statements:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Stäng fönstret!** | Close the window! |
| **Sätt dig här, tack.** | Sit down here, please. |

## Information order and emphasis

Swedish lets speakers choose what to place first to show what the sentence is about or to create a contrast. A time phrase, place phrase, or object can be fronted, but the finite verb remains second:

| Neutral subject-first order | Fronted element | English |
|---|---|---|
| **Vi träffade Sara på stationen.** | **På stationen träffade vi Sara.** | We met Sara at the station. / At the station we met Sara. |
| **Jag läser den här tidningen.** | **Den här tidningen läser jag varje morgon.** | I read this newspaper. / I read this newspaper every morning. |

Fronting can change the focus or contrast, even when the basic event stays the same. Do not move **inte** freely in the same way: its position is tied to whether the clause is main or subordinate.

## A practical check

1. Find the clause's finite verb. In a phrase such as **har läst**, **har** is finite; in **ska läsa**, **ska** is finite.
2. Decide whether the clause is a main clause, a direct question, or a subordinate clause.
3. In a statement main clause, put the finite verb second. If another element comes first, place the subject after that verb.
4. In a subordinate clause, put the subject before the finite verb and place sentence adverbs such as **inte** before it.
5. In a yes-or-no question, put the finite verb before the subject. In a direct question-word question, put the question word first and the finite verb next.

> **I dag kommer hon inte eftersom hon inte mår bra.** — Today she is not coming because she is not feeling well.

The main clause begins with **I dag**, so **kommer** follows it and comes before **hon**; **inte** follows the finite verb. In the subordinate clause beginning with **eftersom**, **inte** comes before the finite verb **mår**.
