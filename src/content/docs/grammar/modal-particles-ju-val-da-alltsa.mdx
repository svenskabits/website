Small words such as **ju**, **väl**, **då** and **alltså** can show how a speaker relates to what they are saying: what they think the listener knows, how certain they are, or what response they expect. In these uses, they are often called **modal particles**. They do not change the basic facts in a sentence, but they change its tone and meaning in context.

There is no single English translation for each particle. The best translation depends on the situation, the speaker's intention and the intonation. The particles do not take endings.

## Ju: something you expect the listener to know

**Ju** presents information as known, obvious or already established between the speaker and listener. It can remind someone of something or explain why a conclusion follows.

| Swedish | Possible translation |
| --- | --- |
| Du känner ju Anna. | You know Anna, after all. / You know Anna, of course. |
| Jag sa ju att tåget var försenat. | I did tell you that the train was delayed. |
| Det är ju fredag i dag. | It is Friday today, after all. |

The speaker is not necessarily annoyed or correcting the listener. **Ju** can sound matter-of-fact and friendly; context and intonation determine whether it sounds like a gentle reminder or an impatient one.

In a main clause, **ju** often comes after the finite verb (the verb showing tense): *Du känner ju Anna.* If the clause has **inte**, **ju** normally comes before it: *Du känner ju inte Anna.* — “You don't know Anna, after all.”

## Väl: an expectation or a tentative question

**Väl** often shows that the speaker expects something to be true but leaves room for the listener to confirm or correct it. In a question, it can make the question sound less direct than a plain request for information.

| Swedish | Possible translation |
| --- | --- |
| Du har väl fått mitt mejl? | You got my email, didn't you? |
| Det går väl bra? | That should be fine, right? |
| Du har väl inte glömt nycklarna? | You haven't forgotten the keys, have you? |

It can also make a suggestion sound open and conversational:

> Vi kan väl ta en kaffe efter mötet. — We could have a coffee after the meeting, couldn't we? / How about having a coffee after the meeting?

**Väl** does not always signal uncertainty. In a phrase such as *Det är väl klart!* it can reinforce an obvious answer: “Of course!” In a question, the speaker may be fairly confident, or may genuinely be unsure; the situation and intonation make the difference.

## Då: connecting to the situation or inviting a response

**Då** can refer to a point in time (“then”), or mean “in that case”. As a conversational particle, it can connect a question or suggestion to what has just been said, or encourage the listener to respond. These uses overlap, so the context matters.

| Swedish | Possible translation |
| --- | --- |
| Vad tycker du då? | What do you think, then? |
| Ska vi gå då? | Shall we go, then? |
| Då gör vi så. | In that case, that's what we'll do. |

In an instruction, **då** can encourage someone to act, but depending on the voice it can also sound impatient:

> Berätta då! — Go on, tell me, then!

Compare *Vad gjorde du då?* (“What did you do then?”), which may ask about the next event in a story, with *Vad tycker du då?* (“What do you think, then?”), which asks for the listener's view in the current conversation. Neither **då** nor its English translation has exactly the same force in every context.

## Alltså: drawing a conclusion or rephrasing

**Alltså** can mark a conclusion (“so”, “therefore”) or explain something in different words (“that is”, “in other words”). In conversation it is also used to frame or emphasize a statement, sometimes much like “I mean”.

| Swedish | Possible translation |
| --- | --- |
| Bussen går inte, så vi får alltså gå. | The bus isn't running, so we'll have to walk, then. |
| Hon är min kusin, alltså min mammas systers dotter. | She's my cousin—that is, my mother's sister's daughter. |
| Alltså, jag vet inte vad vi ska göra. | I mean, I don't know what we're going to do. |

As a conclusion marker, **alltså** can appear near the start or within a clause. The basic word order still applies:

> Det är alltså inte möjligt att boka i dag. — So it isn't possible to book today.

Here **alltså** comes after the finite verb *är*. At the start of a spoken turn, it is often followed by a pause, as in *Alltså, jag vet inte.* Its tone can be neutral, emphatic or frustrated; the word alone does not tell you which.

## Position and tone

In ordinary main clauses, these particles often occur in the middle of the clause, after the finite verb and before **inte** or another part of the verb phrase:

| Swedish | English |
| --- | --- |
| Hon kommer ju inte i kväll. | She's not coming tonight, as you know. |
| Du har väl inte sett min telefon? | You haven't seen my phone, have you? |

This is a useful pattern, not a rule that fixes every particle in one position. **Alltså** can also introduce a turn, and **då** can appear at the end of a question or instruction. Word order and emphasis can change with the sentence and the speaker's intended focus.

Modal particles are especially common in conversation. They are not interchangeable: **ju** points to shared or assumed knowledge, **väl** presents an expectation that may be checked, **då** links the words to the situation or prompts a response, and **alltså** draws a conclusion or reformulates an idea. Listen to the whole sentence—including the intonation—and the surrounding conversation to interpret them.

Do not confuse every occurrence of these words with a modal particle. **Då** can simply mean “then” in a sequence, and **alltså** can explicitly mean “therefore” or “that is”. **Ju** in the pattern *ju … desto …* means “the … the …” rather than acting as the conversational particle:

> Ju mer jag tränar, desto lättare blir det. — The more I practice, the easier it gets.
