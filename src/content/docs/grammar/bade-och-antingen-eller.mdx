## Two paired conjunctions

**Både … och** means **both … and**. It includes the two (or more) things named: they are presented together as true, wanted, involved, or relevant.

**Antingen … eller** means **either … or**. It presents alternatives and asks the listener to consider one option or the other. The alternatives may be mutually exclusive in context, but the phrase does not by itself guarantee that exactly one is possible; the context supplies that meaning.

The first word points forward to the first alternative, and the second word introduces the matching alternative:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| *Vi kan både cykla och ta bussen.* | We can both cycle and take the bus. |
| *Vi kan antingen cykla eller ta bussen.* | We can either cycle or take the bus. |

The two parts should have a clear, parallel relationship. They can be words, phrases, or whole clauses.

## Coordinating words and phrases

Use the pair around two words of the same kind, such as nouns, adjectives, or verbs:

- *Hon talar både svenska och finska.* — She speaks both Swedish and Finnish.
- *Förslaget är både dyrt och svårt att genomföra.* — The proposal is both expensive and difficult to carry out.
- *De vill antingen sälja huset eller hyra ut det.* — They want either to sell the house or rent it out.

The first part can also introduce a longer phrase. Keep the two alternatives parallel so that it is clear what is being compared:

- *Både min syster och hennes sambo arbetar inom vården.* — Both my sister and her partner work in healthcare.
- *Du kan antingen ta ledigt på fredag eller arbeta hemifrån.* — You can either take Friday off or work from home.
- *Kommunen satsar både på kollektivtrafik och på fler cykelvägar.* — The municipality is investing both in public transport and in more cycle paths.

Repeating a small connecting word in both alternatives can make the parallel structure easier to follow, as in *både på kollektivtrafik och på fler cykelvägar*. It is not necessary when the relationship is already clear.

## Coordinating clauses

The alternatives can each be a complete clause. Each clause keeps its own ordinary word order:

- *Både regeringen föreslog en ny reform och oppositionen presenterade ett motförslag.* — The government proposed a new reform, and the opposition also presented a counterproposal.
- *Antingen stannar vi hemma, eller så går vi på bio.* — Either we stay at home, or we go to the cinema.
- *Antingen har hon glömt mötet, eller också har hon missförstått tiden.* — Either she has forgotten the meeting, or she has misunderstood the time.

In the first example, **både** and **och** join two clauses; the subject comes before the verb in each. In the either-or examples, *så* and *också* are possible additions after *eller* that help introduce the second clause. They do not change its basic clause order.

## Position and scope

Put **både** immediately before the first element it applies to, and put **och** before the matching second element. This placement tells the reader which parts belong together:

- *Hon både undervisar och skriver läromedel.* — She both teaches and writes teaching materials.
- *Hon undervisar både vuxna och ungdomar.* — She teaches both adults and young people.
- *Både hennes undervisning och hennes läromedel är uppskattade.* — Both her teaching and her teaching materials are appreciated.

Moving **både** can change the emphasis or the scope (the part of the sentence it applies to). In speech, stress also helps make the intended pairing clear. If a sentence could be read in more than one way, move the pair closer to the elements being contrasted or rephrase it.

**Antingen** is likewise placed before the first alternative. When the paired phrase begins a main clause as an adverbial, the finite verb normally follows it to keep the verb in the second position:

- *Antingen åker vi tidigt eller så tar vi en senare buss.* — Either we leave early, or we take a later bus.

Here *antingen* is the first element of the clause, so *åker* comes before the subject *vi*. But when the alternatives together form the subject, the subject stays before the verb:

- *Antingen tåget eller bussen passar bäst.* — Either the train or the bus works best.

## Agreement and reference

Swedish finite verbs do not change form to show singular or plural subjects. A subject joined with **både … och** can refer to more than one person or thing, but the verb form remains the same:

- *Både läraren och eleverna arbetar med förslaget.* — Both the teacher and the students are working on the proposal.
- *Både dörren och fönstret var öppna.* — Both the door and the window were open.

In the second sentence, the predicative adjective *öppna* has plural form because it describes the two things together. With **antingen … eller**, the sentence presents alternatives rather than a combined subject. If the alternatives would call for different adjective forms or make a later pronoun unclear, repeat the description or reformulate the sentence so the intended reference is unmistakable.

## Choosing the right pair

Use **både … och** when you mean that both alternatives apply. Use **antingen … eller** when you are presenting a choice or alternatives:

- *Både bostadspriserna och räntorna har stigit.* — Both housing prices and interest rates have risen.
- *Antingen höjer banken räntan eller så blir det svårare att få lån.* — Either the bank raises the interest rate, or it becomes harder to get a loan.

The intended meaning can depend on context. A speaker may use *antingen … eller* to frame a practical choice even when both options could in principle be combined. If it matters that only one option is allowed, say so explicitly:

- *Du får välja antingen te eller kaffe, men inte båda.* — You may choose either tea or coffee, but not both.
