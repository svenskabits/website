Use **att** to introduce a clause that tells us what someone thinks, knows, says, hopes, or feels. In this use, **att** means **that**. The clause it introduces is a subordinate clause: it depends on another part of the sentence.

> Jag tror att hon bor i Malmö.  
> I think that she lives in Malmö.

> Han säger att han är trött.  
> He says that he is tired.

> Vi hoppas att du kommer i kväll.  
> We hope that you are coming tonight.

## Word order inside an att-clause

In a statement, an att-clause usually has this order:

**att + subject + sentence adverb + finite verb + the rest**

The **finite verb** is the verb that carries the tense, or the first verb in a verb group. Sentence adverbs include **inte** (not), **aldrig** (never), and **ofta** (often). In a subordinate clause, these adverbs come before the finite verb. This is different from an ordinary main clause, where **inte** usually comes after the finite verb.

| Main clause | Att-clause |
|---|---|
| Hon kommer inte i dag. — She is not coming today. | Jag vet att hon inte kommer i dag. — I know that she is not coming today. |
| Han har aldrig varit där. — He has never been there. | Hon säger att han aldrig har varit där. — She says that he has never been there. |
| De kan ofta hjälpa oss. — They can often help us. | Jag tror att de ofta kan hjälpa oss. — I think that they can often help us. |

The same order applies when there is more than one verb. **Inte** comes before the finite verb, while the other verb or verbs follow it:

> Jag vet att hon inte har läst mejlet.  
> I know that she has not read the email.

> De tror att vi inte kan stanna länge.  
> They think that we cannot stay long.

Without a sentence adverb, the subject still comes before the verb:

> Jag vet att Erik arbetar hemma.  
> I know that Erik works from home.

Do not use the main-clause verb-before-subject order inside the att-clause:

> **Correct:** Jag tror att hon kommer i morgon. — I think that she is coming tomorrow.  
> **Not:** Jag tror att kommer hon i morgon.

## Where the clause can go

An att-clause often follows the main clause:

> Sara berättar att hon ska flytta.  
> Sara says that she is going to move.

It can also come first. When it does, the following main clause keeps main-clause word order: the finite verb comes before the subject if no other subject comes after the clause.

> Att du mår bättre gör mig glad.  
> That you are feeling better makes me happy.

Here, **att du mår bättre** is the subject of **gör mig glad**. In this example the whole att-clause occupies the subject position, so **gör** follows it.

Another useful pattern is **det + verb/adjective + att-clause**:

> Det är bra att du tränar.  
> It is good that you exercise.

> Det ser ut som att det kommer att regna.  
> It looks as if it is going to rain.

## Att can sometimes be left out

After many verbs of thinking, knowing, or saying, **att** is often omitted when the att-clause follows the main clause. Both versions are correct:

> Jag tror att bussen är sen. / Jag tror bussen är sen.  
> I think that the bus is late.

> Hon sa att hon var sjuk. / Hon sa hon var sjuk.  
> She said that she was ill.

Keeping **att** is always a clear choice, especially in careful writing. Do not omit it when the clause comes first:

> Att bussen är sen är irriterande.  
> That the bus is late is annoying.

## Att as “that” and att before an infinitive

In an att-clause, **att** is followed by a subject and a finite verb:

> Jag vet att **hon arbetar**.  
> I know that **she works**.

Swedish also uses **att** before an infinitive, the basic form of a verb. This is a different use: it does not introduce a clause with its own subject and finite verb.

> Jag tycker om att **läsa**.  
> I like to read.

> Det är viktigt att **sova** ordentligt.  
> It is important to sleep properly.

After modal verbs such as **kan**, **vill**, and **måste**, the infinitive normally appears without **att**:

> Jag vill **lära** mig svenska.  
> I want to learn Swedish.

Look for a subject and a finite verb after **att** to recognize the “that” clause: **att hon arbetar**. If **att** is followed directly by an infinitive, as in **att läsa**, it is the infinitive marker instead.
