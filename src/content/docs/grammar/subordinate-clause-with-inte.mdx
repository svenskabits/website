In a Swedish **subordinate clause** (a clause that depends on another clause), *inte* usually comes before the finite verb. The finite verb is the verb that shows tense, or the first verb in a verb phrase. This word order is different from the usual order in a main clause.

## The usual word order

In a subordinate clause, the subject comes first, then *inte*, then the finite verb:

**subordinator + subject + inte + finite verb + rest**

> Jag stannar hemma eftersom **jag inte mår** bra.
> *I'm staying home because I'm not feeling well.*

The subordinate clause starts with *eftersom* (“because”). Its subject is *jag*, and *inte* comes before the finite verb *mår*.

This pattern applies with many subordinators, including *att* (that), *eftersom* (because), *när* (when), and *om* (if/whether):

| Subordinator | Swedish | English |
|---|---|---|
| *att* | Jag tror att hon inte arbetar i dag. | I think that she isn't working today. |
| *eftersom* | Vi tog en taxi eftersom bussen inte kom. | We took a taxi because the bus didn't come. |
| *när* | Ring mig när du inte är upptagen. | Call me when you aren't busy. |
| *om* | Jag undrar om de inte har fått mejlet. | I wonder whether they haven't received the email. |

In the last example, the first *inte* belongs to the main clause (*Jag vet inte*); the second belongs to the subordinate clause (*om de inte har fått mejlet*).

## With more than one verb

When a subordinate clause has a verb phrase, *inte* usually comes before its first, finite verb. The other verbs follow it:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| Jag vet att hon inte kan komma. | I know that she can't come. |
| Han sa att han inte hade sett filmen. | He said that he hadn't seen the film. |
| De hoppas att tåget inte blir försenat. | They hope that the train won't be delayed. |

Here, *kan*, *hade*, and *blir* are the finite verbs. Do not put *inte* between the verbs in these examples: the usual order is *inte kan komma*, *inte hade sett*, and *inte blir försenat*.

## Compare a subordinate clause with a main clause

In an ordinary main clause, *inte* usually follows the finite verb. In a subordinate clause, it usually comes before it:

| Main clause | Subordinate clause |
|---|---|
| Hon kommer inte i dag. — *She isn't coming today.* | … eftersom hon inte kommer i dag. — *… because she isn't coming today.* |
| Han har inte ringt. — *He hasn't called.* | … fast han inte har ringt. — *… although he hasn't called.* |

The position of the subordinate clause in the sentence does not change its word order:

> Eftersom hon inte mår bra går hon hem.
> *Because she isn't feeling well, she is going home.*
>
> Hon går hem eftersom hon inte mår bra.
> *She is going home because she isn't feeling well.*

Even when the subordinate clause comes first, *inte* still comes before its finite verb *mår*. The main clause follows its own word order: *går hon*.

## In indirect questions and relative clauses

An indirect question is a question inside another clause. It uses subordinate word order:

> Jag undrar varför hon inte svarar.
> *I wonder why she isn't answering.*
>
> Vet du varför butiken inte har öppet på söndagar?
> *Do you know why the shop isn't open on Sundays?*

A relative clause gives information about a noun and also uses subordinate word order:

> Jag har en kollega som inte dricker kaffe.
> *I have a colleague who doesn't drink coffee.*

In *som inte dricker kaffe*, *som* refers to *en kollega*. The subject is followed by *inte* and then the finite verb *dricker*.

## Do not confuse a finite subordinate clause with an infinitive phrase

The rule above applies to subordinate clauses with a finite verb. A phrase with an infinitive is different: *inte* normally comes immediately before the infinitive, often after *att*:

> Hon gick tidigt för att inte missa tåget.
> *She left early so as not to miss the train.*

Compare it with a finite subordinate clause, which has its own subject and finite verb:

> Hon gick tidigt eftersom hon inte ville missa tåget.
> *She left early because she didn't want to miss the train.*

In the first sentence, *inte missa* is an infinitive phrase. In the second, *hon inte ville missa tåget* is a subordinate clause, so *inte* comes before the finite verb *ville*.

## A quick check

When you use *inte* in a sentence:

1. Find the clause containing the negation.
2. Decide whether it is a main clause or a subordinate clause.
3. In a main clause, *inte* usually follows the finite verb. In a finite subordinate clause, it usually comes after the subject and before the finite verb.

> I dag går hon inte till jobbet eftersom hon inte mår bra.
> *Today she isn't going to work because she isn't feeling well.*

The main-clause verb *går* comes before *inte*. In the subordinate clause, *inte* comes before the finite verb *mår*.
