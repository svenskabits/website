The word **om** can introduce a condition (“if”) or an indirect yes/no question (“whether” or “if”). In both uses, **om** begins a subordinate clause, so the word order inside that clause is different from the word order in a main clause.

## **Om** for a condition: “if”

Use **om** to say that one thing will happen only under a certain condition:

> Om det regnar stannar vi hemma.<br />
> If it rains, we will stay at home.

> Jag ringer dig om jag blir sen.<br />
> I will call you if I am late.

> Om du vill kan vi äta nu.<br />
> If you want, we can eat now.

The condition can come before or after the main clause, as these examples show.

## Word order in an **om**-clause

An **om**-clause has subordinate-clause word order. The usual pattern is:

**om + subject + (sentence adverb) + finite verb + the rest**

Sentence adverbs include **inte** (“not”), **kanske** (“perhaps”), and **alltid** (“always”). In a subordinate clause, **inte** comes before the finite verb:

> Om jag inte hittar nycklarna tar jag bussen.<br />
> If I don't find the keys, I will take the bus.

> Vi går en promenad om det inte regnar.<br />
> We will go for a walk if it doesn't rain.

Compare the subordinate clause with a main clause:

| Main clause | **Om**-clause |
|---|---|
| Det regnar inte. — It is not raining. | om det inte regnar — if it is not raining |
| Hon kommer alltid i tid. — She is always on time. | om hon alltid kommer i tid — if she is always on time |

When an **om**-clause comes first, the following main clause uses main-clause word order. The verb comes before the subject because the clause already occupies the first position:

> Om bussen är sen tar jag tåget.<br />
> If the bus is late, I will take the train.

Do not use question word order inside the **om**-clause:

> **Correct:** Om **hon kommer** kan vi börja. — If she comes, we can start.
>
> **Not:** Om **kommer hon** kan vi börja.

## **Om** in an indirect yes/no question: “whether”

Use **om** when you report or ask about a question that can be answered with **ja** (“yes”) or **nej** (“no”). In English, this often means **whether** or **if**:

> Jag vet inte om affären är öppen.<br />
> I don't know whether the shop is open.

> Hon frågade om jag kunde hjälpa henne.<br />
> She asked if I could help her.

> Kan du se om tåget är på väg?<br />
> Can you see whether the train is on its way?

Inside the indirect question, use subordinate-clause order: the subject comes before the verb, and **inte** comes before the finite verb. Do not use the verb-before-subject order of a direct yes/no question.

| Direct question | Indirect question |
|---|---|
| Är bussen sen? — Is the bus late? | Jag undrar om bussen är sen. — I wonder whether the bus is late. |
| Kommer hon inte i dag? — Isn't she coming today? | Vet du om hon inte kommer i dag? — Do you know if she isn't coming today? |

An indirect question is not itself a question just because it contains **om**. Use a question mark only if the whole sentence is a question:

> Jag vet inte om han är hemma.<br />
> I don't know if he is at home.

> Vet du om han är hemma?<br />
> Do you know if he is at home?

## “If” or “whether”?

The meaning comes from the context. If the **om**-clause describes a condition for something to happen, **om** means **if**:

> Om du har tid kan du ringa mig.<br />
> If you have time, you can call me.

If someone does not know or asks whether something is true, **om** introduces an indirect question and means **whether** or **if**:

> Jag vet inte om hon har tid.<br />
> I don't know whether she has time.

To make “whether or not” explicit, Swedish can use **om … eller inte**:

> Vi går ut oavsett om det regnar eller inte.<br />
> We are going out, whether it rains or not.

In an indirect question, **om … eller inte** can also mean “whether or not”:

> Hon frågade om vi ville följa med eller inte.<br />
> She asked whether we wanted to come along or not.

## Other meanings of **om**

**Om** has other uses too. For example, it can mean “about” before a topic:

> Vi pratade om resan.<br />
> We talked about the trip.

This is a preposition, not the conjunction that introduces a condition or an indirect yes/no question. Use the sentence structure to recognize the difference: a conjunction **om** is followed by a subject and a verb, as in **om hon kommer** (“if/whether she comes”).
