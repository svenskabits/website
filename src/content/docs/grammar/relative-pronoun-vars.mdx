## Meaning and form

**Vars** is a relative pronoun in the genitive and means **whose** or, for things, **of which**. It links a noun to a relative clause that tells us about something belonging to or associated with that noun.

The noun that *vars* refers back to is called its **antecedent**. *Vars* itself does not change for the antecedent's gender or number, or for the number of the noun after it. That following noun is singular or plural as needed. *Vars* is used with people, animals, things, and organizations:

- *Jag träffade en kvinna vars son bor i Uppsala.* — I met a woman whose son lives in Uppsala.
- *De anställda vars barn går på förskola får gå tidigare.* — The employees whose children attend preschool may leave earlier.
- *Vi besökte ett hus vars tak var täckt av snö.* — We visited a house whose roof was covered in snow.
- *Företaget vars produkter säljs över hela världen öppnar en ny fabrik.* — The company whose products are sold all over the world is opening a new factory.

## Where *vars* goes

Put *vars* immediately before the noun or noun phrase for the person or thing that is possessed or associated with the antecedent:

> antecedent + **vars** + possessed noun phrase + rest of the relative clause

- *Hon har en bror vars fru arbetar som läkare.* — She has a brother whose wife works as a doctor.
- *Det finns flera kommuner vars skolor behöver renoveras.* — There are several municipalities whose schools need renovating.
- *Jag läste en artikel vars huvudargument var svårt att följa.* — I read an article whose main argument was difficult to follow.

The noun after *vars* is normally in the indefinite form, as with other Swedish possessive words: *vars bil*, *vars barn*, *vars gamla hus*. Do not add the definite ending to that noun: *vars bil*, not *vars bilen*.

The relative clause can describe the antecedent's possession in different ways. The *vars*-phrase may be the subject of the clause:

- *Mannen vars bil inte startar väntar på hjälp.* — The man whose car will not start is waiting for help.
- *En författare vars senaste bok har blivit översatt kommer till biblioteket.* — An author whose latest book has been translated is coming to the library.

Or it may be the object, while another subject comes before the verb:

- *Mannen vars bil jag lånade bor nära stationen.* — The man whose car I borrowed lives near the station.
- *Vi pratade med en forskare vars arbete många känner till.* — We spoke with a researcher whose work many people know about.
- *Hon fick stöd av en organisation vars verksamhet hon är stolt över.* — She received support from an organization whose work she is proud of.

*Vars* needs an antecedent and a noun that names what is associated with it; it does not stand alone in place of a noun. In the example *en kvinna vars son ...*, *vars* points back to *en kvinna*, while *son* names the person related to her.

## Word order inside the relative clause

A relative clause is a subordinate clause. If its subject is the *vars*-phrase, ordinary subordinate-clause word order puts a sentence adverb such as *inte* before the finite verb:

- *Jag känner en lärare vars barn inte går i skolan här.* — I know a teacher whose children do not go to school here.
- *De flyttade till ett hus vars värmesystem inte fungerade.* — They moved to a house whose heating system did not work.

If the *vars*-phrase is an object and another subject is present, that subject comes before the adverb and verb:

- *Hon har en vän vars råd jag aldrig glömmer.* — She has a friend whose advice I will never forget.
- *Vi hittade ett företag vars namn jag inte kunde uttala.* — We found a company whose name I could not pronounce.

The word order does not change just because the relative clause comes at the end of a sentence:

- *Jag känner en man vars dotter spelar i landslaget.* — I know a man whose daughter plays on the national team.

## *Vars* and *vems*

Use **vems** mainly to ask a direct or indirect question about the owner. Use **vars** to connect a relative clause to a noun already mentioned:

- *Vems jacka ligger på stolen?* — Whose jacket is on the chair?
- *Jag vet inte vems jacka det är.* — I do not know whose jacket it is.
- *Jag pratade med personen vars jacka låg på stolen.* — I spoke with the person whose jacket was on the chair.

Notice the word order in the indirect question *vems jacka det är*: it is a subordinate clause, so the subject *det* comes before the verb *är*.

**Vilkens** is a genitive form of *vilken*, but it is uncommon in ordinary contemporary Swedish. When asking which person's something is, Swedish speakers generally use *vems* or the clearer phrase *vilken persons*; *vars* is not used to ask the question:

- *Vilken persons pass hittade du?* — Which person's passport did you find?
- *Passet tillhör personen vars väska vi hittade.* — The passport belongs to the person whose bag we found.

## Choosing *vars* or another relative construction

Use *vars* when the relationship is possessive or closely associated. Use *som* when the antecedent itself is the subject or object of the relative clause:

- *Kvinnan som bor bredvid oss är läkare.* — The woman who lives next door to us is a doctor.
- *Kvinnan vars son är läkare bor bredvid oss.* — The woman whose son is a doctor lives next door to us.
- *Boken som jag lånade var spännande.* — The book that I borrowed was exciting.
- *Boken vars omslag är blå ligger på bordet.* — The book whose cover is blue is on the table.

Do not use *vars* as a general replacement for *som*. In the last example, it is the cover—not the book—that is blue, and *vars* expresses the relationship between the book and its cover.

Relative clauses that identify which person or thing is meant are normally written without commas. A comma pair can mark a clause as extra, non-essential information:

- *Vi söker personer vars erfarenhet passar tjänsten.* — We are looking for people whose experience suits the position.
- *Maja, vars erfarenhet passar tjänsten, börjar på måndag.* — Maja, whose experience suits the position, starts on Monday.

In both cases *vars* refers to the noun before it; the commas only show how the added information relates to the rest of the sentence.
