An object pronoun stands for the person or thing that receives an action, or that follows a preposition. Swedish uses different forms for subject pronouns and object pronouns: **jag** means “I,” while **mig** means “me.”

| Subject | Object | Example | Translation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| jag (I) | mig (me) | Hon ser mig. | She sees me. |
| du (you, singular) | dig (you) | Jag känner dig. | I know you. |
| han (he) | honom (him) | Vi väntar på honom. | We are waiting for him. |
| hon (she) | henne (her) | Jag hjälper henne. | I help her. |
| den (it; an en-word) | den (it) | Jag köper den. | I am buying it. |
| det (it; an ett-word) | det (it) | Jag hittar det. | I find it. |
| vi (we) | oss (us) | De hör oss. | They hear us. |
| ni (you, plural) | er (you) | Jag ser er. | I see you. |
| de (they) | dem (them) | Han känner dem. | He knows them. |

The object forms **den** and **det** refer to nouns according to their grammatical gender: **den** for an *en*-word and **det** for an *ett*-word. The form does not depend on whether the English translation uses “he,” “she” or “it.”

## Objects after verbs

In a simple statement, an object pronoun normally comes after the verb:

- **Jag älskar dig.** — I love you.
- **Hon ringde mig i går.** — She called me yesterday.
- **Kan du hjälpa oss?** — Can you help us?

The same pronouns are used for direct and indirect objects. A direct object is what someone sees, buys or reads; an indirect object is often the person who receives something. When both objects are present, the person commonly comes first:

- **Hon gav mig en kopp kaffe.** — She gave me a cup of coffee.
- **Jag skickade henne ett meddelande.** — I sent her a message.

You can also use **till** (“to”) before the recipient, especially when the thing comes first:

- **Hon gav en kopp kaffe till mig.** — She gave a cup of coffee to me.
- **Jag skickade ett meddelande till henne.** — I sent a message to her.

## After prepositions

Use the object form after a preposition such as **med** (with), **för** (for), **till** (to), **från** (from) or **på** in expressions such as **vänta på** (wait for):

- **Vill du följa med mig?** — Do you want to come with me?
- **Den här presenten är till dig.** — This present is for you.
- **Vi väntar på dem.** — We are waiting for them.
- **Han pratade om henne.** — He talked about her.

The preposition stays before the pronoun, even when the preposition is part of a verb expression, as in **tycka om någon** (“like someone”):

- **Jag tycker om honom.** — I like him.

## Position with negation and other verbs

In a main clause with one ordinary verb, the object pronoun usually comes before **inte**:

- **Jag känner honom inte.** — I do not know him.
- **De såg oss inte.** — They did not see us.

Putting **inte** before the object can give the sentence a contrastive emphasis, as in “I don’t know *him* (but someone else).”

When a sentence has a modal or auxiliary verb, **inte** normally follows the finite verb, and the object pronoun stays with the main verb:

- **Jag kan inte hjälpa dig.** — I cannot help you.
- **Hon har inte träffat dem.** — She has not met them.

In a subordinate clause, **inte** comes before the verb, while the object pronoun follows the verb:

- **Jag vet att hon inte känner honom.** — I know that she does not know him.

In a command, put the object pronoun after the verb:

- **Ring mig i kväll!** — Call me tonight!
- **Hjälp oss, tack!** — Help us, please!

## De, dem and dom

In standard written Swedish, **de** is the subject form (“they”) and **dem** is the object form (“them”):

- **De känner mig.** — They know me.
- **Jag känner dem.** — I know them.

Both are normally pronounced **dom** in everyday speech. **Dom** is also common in informal writing, but **de** and **dem** are the usual choices in formal writing and in this course.

## Object pronouns and reflexive pronouns

Use an ordinary object pronoun when the object is someone or something other than the subject. Use a reflexive pronoun when the object refers back to the subject. The third-person reflexive form is **sig**, not **honom**, **henne** or **dem**:

- **Erik ser honom.** — Erik sees him (another man).
- **Erik ser sig i spegeln.** — Erik sees himself in the mirror.
- **Anna hjälper dem.** — Anna helps them.
- **Anna hjälper sig själv.** — Anna helps herself.

The forms **mig**, **dig**, **oss** and **er** can be ordinary object pronouns or reflexive pronouns; the meaning depends on whether they refer to the subject. For example, **Jag tvättar mig** means “I wash myself,” while **Hon tvättar mig** means “She washes me.”

For a person who uses the gender-neutral pronoun **hen**, **hen** can also be used as an object form; **henom** is another form some speakers use. Choose the form that the person uses for themselves.
