Swedish often puts the subject first, but a sentence can begin with another part of the sentence when the speaker wants to make it the starting point or give it extra prominence. This is called **fronting**. In a main clause, fronting a non-subject does not change the verb-second rule: the finite verb still comes second.

## The basic pattern

When something other than the subject comes first, the usual order is:

> **Fronted element + finite verb + subject + rest of the clause**

The **finite verb** is the verb marked for tense, or the first verb in a verb phrase. The subject normally follows it in this pattern. Compare the neutral order with a fronted time phrase:

| Subject first | Fronted element |
| --- | --- |
| Jag läste boken i går. — I read the book yesterday. | I går läste jag boken. — Yesterday I read the book. |
| Vi träffade Sara på stationen. — We met Sara at the station. | På stationen träffade vi Sara. — At the station we met Sara. |
| Hon har bott här i tio år. — She has lived here for ten years. | Här har hon bott i tio år. — Here she has lived for ten years. |

The fronted element can be one word or a longer phrase. It takes the first position as a unit; the finite verb follows it:

> **Efter det långa mötet** gick vi och åt lunch. — **After the long meeting**, we went to have lunch.

## Fronting different kinds of elements

### Time, place and other adverbials

Time and place phrases are often fronted to set the scene or connect the sentence to what was just said:

| Subject first | Fronted element |
| --- | --- |
| Vi brukar bada i sjön på sommaren. — We usually swim in the lake in summer. | På sommaren brukar vi bada i sjön. — In summer we usually swim in the lake. |
| Bussen går från den här hållplatsen. — The bus leaves from this stop. | Från den här hållplatsen går bussen. — The bus leaves from this stop. |
| Det tar ungefär en timme med bil. — It takes about an hour by car. | Med bil tar det ungefär en timme. — By car it takes about an hour. |

The first example has **brukar** as its finite verb; the infinitive **bada** stays later in the verb phrase. The verb-second rule concerns the finite verb, not every verb in the sentence.

### Objects

An object can be placed first to make it the topic or to contrast it with something else. The subject then follows the finite verb:

| Subject first | Fronted object |
| --- | --- |
| Jag har redan läst den boken. — I have already read that book. | Den boken har jag redan läst. — That book, I have already read. |
| Vi förstår inte det här beslutet. — We don't understand this decision. | Det här beslutet förstår vi inte. — This decision, we don't understand. |
| Hon valde den röda klänningen, inte den blå. — She chose the red dress, not the blue one. | Den röda klänningen valde hon, inte den blå. — The red dress is what she chose, not the blue one. |

The object is not the subject just because it comes first. In **Den boken har jag redan läst**, **den boken** is what was read, while **jag** is the person who read it.

### Adverbs and negative expressions

Fronting an adverb can make it especially prominent. The same verb-second pattern applies:

> Kanske har hon glömt nycklarna. — Perhaps she has forgotten the keys.  
> Aldrig har jag sett något liknande. — Never have I seen anything like it.  
> Inte ens då förstod jag vad han menade. — Not even then did I understand what he meant.

Putting a negative expression such as **aldrig** (“never”) first is more emphatic than keeping it in its usual position:

> Jag har aldrig sett något liknande. — I have never seen anything like it.

The fronted version is natural when the speaker wants to stress the extent of the experience, but it can sound dramatic or rhetorical. Use it when that emphasis fits the situation.

## The position of **inte**

When an object or adverbial is fronted, **inte** usually remains after the subject in a main clause:

> Den här filmen har jag inte sett. — I haven't seen this film.  
> I dag kan vi inte stanna länge. — We can't stay long today.  
> På grund av vädret åkte de inte till stranden. — Because of the weather, they didn't go to the beach.

In the first two sentences, the finite verbs are **har** and **kan**. The subject (**jag**, **vi**) follows each one, and **inte** comes after the subject. If a negative phrase is fronted for strong contrast, the finite verb follows it and then the subject:

> Inte den blå klänningen valde hon, utan den röda. — She chose not the blue dress, but the red one.

This order is strongly marked and works best when the speaker is setting up a clear contrast. In ordinary conversation, **Hon valde inte den blå klänningen, utan den röda** (“She didn't choose the blue dress, but the red one”) is a more neutral option.

## A subordinate clause at the beginning

A whole subordinate clause can come first in a sentence. It forms the opening element, and the following main clause keeps verb-second order:

> När jag kom hem var maten redan kall. — When I got home, the food was already cold.  
> Om du har tid kan vi ta en kaffe. — If you have time, we can have a coffee.  
> Trots att det regnade tog de en lång promenad. — Although it was raining, they went for a long walk.

After the initial subordinate clause, the finite verb in the main clause comes before its subject: **var maten**, **kan vi**, **gick de**. The subordinate clause itself keeps its own word order. For example, after **när** (“when”), its subject comes before its verb: **När jag kom hem** (“When I got home”).

## Fronting is about focus and flow

Fronting does not always mean strong emotional emphasis. It can:

- establish a time, place or condition before giving the main information;
- connect the sentence to something already mentioned;
- contrast one choice with another;
- give the fronted information extra stress or importance.

For example, **I morgon åker vi till Uppsala** (“Tomorrow we're going to Uppsala”) simply sets the time first. With a strong spoken stress on **i morgon**, the speaker can contrast tomorrow with another day. Context and intonation help show how strong the emphasis is.

Fronting keeps the clause structure and moves one element to the opening position. It is different from building a sentence around a focus expression such as **Det är kaffe jag vill ha** (“Coffee is what I want”), which uses a different grammatical structure.

## A useful check

When a main clause begins with something other than its subject, find the finite verb and check that it comes immediately after that opening element. Then look for the subject after the verb:

> **I kväll** **ska** **vi** laga middag hemma. — **Tonight** **we're going to** cook dinner at home.

Here **i kväll** is the fronted time phrase, **ska** is the finite verb, and **vi** is the subject. The other verb, **laga**, follows later in the verb phrase.
