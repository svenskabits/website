A Swedish possessive tells you who something belongs to. Its form usually matches the **thing owned** (the noun), not the owner. For example, the speaker says **min bok** but **mitt hus** because *bok* is an en-word and *hus* is an ett-word.

## The forms

The table shows the forms used before a noun. The examples in each row show the same possessive with an en-word, an ett-word, and a plural noun.

| Owner | En-word, singular | Ett-word, singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|---|
| **jag** (I) | **min** bok (my book) | **mitt** hus (my house) | **mina** böcker (my books) |
| **du** (you, one person) | **din** bok (your book) | **ditt** hus (your house) | **dina** böcker (your books) |
| **han** (he) | **hans** bok (his book) | **hans** hus (his house) | **hans** böcker (his books) |
| **hon** (she) | **hennes** bok (her book) | **hennes** hus (her house) | **hennes** böcker (her books) |
| **den/det** (it) | **dess** färg (its colour) | **dess** tak (its roof) | **dess** fönster (its windows) |
| **vi** (we) | **vår** bok (our book) | **vårt** hus (our house) | **våra** böcker (our books) |
| **ni** (you, more than one person) | **er** bok (your book) | **ert** hus (your house) | **era** böcker (your books) |
| **de** (they) | **deras** bok (their book) | **deras** hus (their house) | **deras** böcker (their books) |
| **Subject owns it** (han, hon, den/det, de, man) | **sin** bok (his/her/its/their/one's own book) | **sitt** hus (his/her/its/their/one's own house) | **sina** böcker (his/her/its/their/one's own books) |
| **man** (one, people in general) | **ens** bok (one's book) | **ens** hus (one's house) | **ens** böcker (one's books) |

**Min/mina**, **din/dina**, **vår/våra**, and **er/era** change with the noun: use the first form with an en-word, the second with an ett-word, and the third with a plural. **Hus** is an ett-word, so it takes *mitt*, *ditt*, *vårt*, or *ert*; **böcker** is plural, so it takes *mina*, *dina*, *våra*, or *era*.

**Hans**, **hennes**, **dess**, **deras**, and **ens** do not change with the noun. **Dess** normally refers to a thing or an animal, not a person. For example, **Vi pratade om huset och dess historia.** means “We talked about the house and its history.” Swedish uses the same **hans** whether the thing owned is an en-word, an ett-word, or plural; the owner's gender does not affect **min**, **din**, **vår**, or **er** either.

## Position and noun form

Put the possessive before the noun. If there is an adjective, the possessive comes first, and the adjective normally has its definite form, often ending in **-a**:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **min nya bok** | my new book |
| **mitt gamla hus** | my old house |
| **mina fina skor** | my nice shoes |

After a possessive before a noun, do not add the noun's definite ending: say **min bok**, not *min boken*. The possessive already makes the noun phrase definite. The same rule applies with an adjective: **min nya bok**, not *min nya boken*.

In some informal or regional speech, a possessive can also come after a definite noun: **boken min** means “my book.” This order is more conversational than the usual **min bok**.

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Här är min bok.** | Here is my book. |
| **Här är boken min.** | Here is my book. (conversational word order) |

## His, her, its, or their own

Use **sin**, **sitt**, or **sina** when the owner is the third-person subject of the same clause and owns the thing being talked about. Choose among these forms by the noun, just as with **min/mitt/mina**. They can refer to a man, a woman, or more than one person; the form itself does not say whether the owner is “his,” “her,” or “their.”

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Erik läser sin bok.** | Erik is reading his own book. |
| **Maja städar sitt rum.** | Maja is cleaning her own room. |
| **Barnen hämtar sina väskor.** | The children are picking up their own bags. |

Use **hans**, **hennes**, or **deras** when the owner is someone other than the subject. These forms stay the same no matter what noun follows them:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Erik läser hennes bok.** | Erik is reading her book (a woman's book, not Erik's). |
| **Maja städar hans rum.** | Maja is cleaning his room (a man's room, not Maja's). |
| **Barnen hämtar deras väskor.** | The children are picking up their bags (belonging to other people). |

The contrast is about who owns the thing, not who is male or female: **sin/sitt/sina** points back to the clause's subject; **hans/hennes/deras** points to another owner. For a first- or second-person subject, use **min/mitt/mina** or **din/ditt/dina**, not **sin/sitt/sina**. With general **man** as the subject, use **sin/sitt/sina** for the subject's own thing:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Jag tar min jacka.** | I am taking my jacket. |
| **Du tar din jacka.** | You are taking your jacket. |
| **Vi tar våra jackor.** | We are taking our jackets. |
| **Man bör ta hand om sin hälsa.** | One should take care of one's health. |

## Using a possessive on its own

A possessive can stand on its own when the noun is already clear. Forms that change with the noun still match the thing owned:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Den här boken är min.** | This book is mine. |
| **Huset är vårt.** | The house is ours. |
| **Skorna är deras.** | The shoes are theirs. |

## Own: egen, eget, egna

Add **egen**, **eget**, or **egna** after a possessive to emphasize “own.” These words match the thing owned: **egen** with an en-word, **eget** with an ett-word, and **egna** with a plural noun. They also appear after **sin/sitt/sina**:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Hon har sin egen bil.** | She has her own car. |
| **Han har sitt eget rum.** | He has his own room. |
| **De har sina egna nycklar.** | They have their own keys. |
| **Jag vill ha en egen kopp.** | I want a cup of my own. |

**Ens** is the possessive form of general **man** (“one” or “people in general”): **Det påverkar ens hälsa.** (It affects one's health.)
