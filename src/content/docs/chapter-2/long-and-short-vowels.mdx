Swedish has nine vowel letters: **a, e, i, o, u, y, å, ä, ö**. In a stressed syllable, each can have a long or a short sound. The difference is not only how long you hold the vowel: its quality—the way it sounds—can also change.

## The basic pattern

In many stressed syllables, the spelling gives you a useful clue:

| Spelling after the stressed vowel | Usual sound | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| One consonant before the next vowel, or no consonant at the end of the word | Long vowel | *sova* (to sleep), *tal* (speech), *bo* (to live) |
| Two consonant letters, or a consonant cluster | Short vowel | *sommar* (summer), *tall* (pine), *fisk* (fish) |

For a long vowel, hold the vowel a little longer. For a short vowel, make the vowel brief and give the following consonant more time when it is doubled. In *tal*, the **a** is long. In *tall*, the **a** is short and the **l** is long. Do not make both sounds long.

A stressed vowel at the end of a word is usually long: *nu* (now), *vi* (we), *ny* (new). A single consonant followed by another vowel often also goes with a long vowel: *sova* (to sleep), *heta* (to be called), *bilen* (the car).

## Long and short examples

These pairs show how changing the vowel length can distinguish words. The words in a pair may also differ in vowel quality.

| Long vowel | Short vowel |
|---|---|
| *tal* (speech, a number) | *tall* (pine) |
| *vet* (know, as in *jag vet*) | *vett* (sense, good judgement) |
| *vit* (white, common-gender form) | *vitt* (white, neuter form) |
| *bok* (book) | *bock* (goat) |
| *ful* (ugly) | *full* (full, or drunk) |
| *by* (village) | *bytt* (changed, exchanged) |
| *hål* (hole) | *håll* (hold; direction) |
| *väg* (road) | *vägg* (wall) |
| *söt* (sweet, common-gender form) | *sött* (sweet, neuter form) |

The spelling clue works for all nine vowel letters, but the examples above are word contrasts, not a complete description of each vowel's quality. Listen to the whole word, especially for **u**, **y**, **å**, **ä**, and **ö**, whose sounds may not match an English vowel closely.

## Listen to the stressed syllable

The length pattern is most useful in a stressed syllable. Stress means that one part of a word is more prominent. In many everyday Swedish words, the first syllable is stressed, as in **som**mar and **bil**en. In longer words, compounds, and borrowed words, stress can fall elsewhere, so first find the stressed part and then use the spelling as a clue.

Compare the vowel and consonant in these words:

- *fin* (nice) has a long **i**; *finns* (exists; is/are) has a short **i** followed by a consonant cluster.
- *mat* (food) has a long **a**; *matt* (dull) has a short **a** and a long **t**.
- *glas* (drinking glass) has a long vowel; *glass* (ice cream) has a short vowel and a long **s**.

## When spelling is only a clue

The pattern is a guide, not a rule that predicts every vowel in every word.

- It applies most reliably to the **stressed** vowel. Unstressed vowels are often short or reduced, even when the spelling might suggest otherwise.
- A consonant cluster after a vowel usually goes with a short vowel: *varm* (warm), *barn* (child), *frukt* (fruit). The cluster may use different letters; it does not have to be a doubled consonant.
- One consonant letter between vowels often signals a long stressed vowel, as in *sova*, but word structure, endings, and less common spellings can affect what you hear.
- In compound words, stress and vowel length belong to the parts of the compound; do not apply the pattern mechanically across the join.
- Loanwords and some less common words do not always follow the usual spelling pattern. Learn their pronunciation with the word.

Vowel quality and exact duration vary somewhat between speakers and regions of Sweden. These differences are normal. The long-versus-short contrast is useful in standard Swedish throughout the country, even when the precise vowel quality is not identical everywhere.

## Practice

Say each word naturally, then notice what happens in its stressed syllable:

1. *bil* (car) — long vowel; *buss* (bus) — short vowel, followed by a doubled consonant.
2. *bo* (to live) — long vowel at the end; *boll* (ball) — short vowel, followed by a doubled consonant.
3. *vila* (to rest) — long first vowel; *vilja* (will; to want) — short first vowel.

When reading a new word, locate its stress, check the consonant spelling after the vowel, and use that as your first guess. Then listen to a reliable pronunciation and learn any exception as part of the word.
