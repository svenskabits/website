Swedish **å**, **ä** and **ö** are three separate vowel letters, and each represents a vowel sound that can distinguish one word from another. The sound depends partly on whether the vowel is long or short. The descriptions and IPA below give common pronunciations in standard Swedish as spoken in Sweden; vowel quality varies somewhat between regions and speakers.

## The three vowel qualities

| Letter | Long sound | Short sound | How to shape the sound |
|---|---|---|---|
| **å** | [oː] | [ɔ] | Round your lips. Keep the tongue toward the back for the long sound. |
| **ä** | [ɛː] | [ɛ] | Keep the lips unrounded; the tongue is toward the front. Open your mouth more than for **e** in many languages. |
| **ö** | [øː] | [œ] | Round your lips while keeping the tongue toward the front. |

The symbol **[ː]** means that the vowel is long. The other IPA symbols are phonetic descriptions, not English spellings. In particular, English does not have an exact equivalent of Swedish **ö**. For **ö**, make the tongue position for **ä** and round your lips; for **å**, round the lips with the tongue farther back. These are useful starting positions, not a claim that every speaker makes exactly the same sound.

The short and long versions are related, but they are not always just the same sound held for less time: the mouth shape can shift slightly too. The table gives broad IPA transcriptions for a common central-Swedish pronunciation. Regional pronunciations may be more open, more closed, or otherwise different while still keeping the words understandable and the vowel categories distinct.

## Long and short vowels in words

In a stressed syllable, Swedish usually has either a long vowel followed by a short consonant, or a short vowel followed by a long consonant or a consonant cluster. The spelling often gives you a clue:

| Pattern | Examples | What to notice |
|---|---|---|
| Vowel before one consonant, or at the end of a word | **får** (gets; sheep), **söt** (sweet), **blå** (blue) | The stressed vowel is long: [foːr], [søːt], [bloː]. |
| Vowel before a doubled consonant | **fått** (got), **sött** (sweet, used with *ett* words), **blått** (blue, used with *ett* words) | The vowel is short and the consonant is held longer: [fɔtː], [sœtː], [blɔtː]. |

In the transcriptions, **[ː]** after a consonant marks a long consonant. You do not pronounce a doubled consonant as two separate consonants with a break; hold it a little longer. The pairs **får–fått**, **söt–sött** and **blå–blått** show how vowel length and consonant length can change with the word form.

This spelling pattern is a guide, not a rule that works mechanically for every word. A short vowel can also come before a consonant cluster, as in **höst** (autumn), **äpple** (apple) and **måndag** (Monday). Stress, word structure and familiar spelling patterns all matter. In unstressed syllables, vowels are generally less prominent, so the long-versus-short contrast is clearest in stressed syllables.

## Hearing and saying each letter

### Å

For long **å**, round your lips and make a steady vowel toward the back of the mouth. Common examples include:

- **gå** — to go
- **år** — year
- **blå** — blue
- **får** — gets; sheep
- **åka** — to travel, go by vehicle

Short **å** has a more open quality in many standard-Swedish pronunciations. Listen for it in **fått** (got), **blått** (blue, used with *ett* words), **åtta** (eight) and **måndag** (Monday). In **åtta**, the doubled **tt** is a clue to the short vowel.

### Ä

For **ä**, keep your lips unrounded and place the tongue toward the front. The long vowel is heard in **äta** (to eat), **läsa** (to read), **här** (here) and **värld** (world). The short vowel occurs in **äpple** (apple), **rätt** (right; correct), **lätt** (easy; light) and **vänta** (to wait).

### Ö

For **ö**, round your lips as you would for **å**, but keep the tongue toward the front of the mouth. The long vowel occurs in **öl** (beer), **öga** (eye), **söt** (sweet) and **snö** (snow). Short **ö** occurs in **sött** (sweet, used with *ett* words), **mössa** (hat), **född** (born) and **höst** (autumn).

## Keep the vowels distinct

Listen for both tongue position and lip shape. **Å** is rounded and farther back; **ö** is rounded and farther forward; **ä** is unrounded and toward the front. Then listen for vowel length and the consonant after it. Practise these words in pairs, first slowly and then at a natural pace:

| Long vowel | Short vowel | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **får** [foːr] | **fått** [fɔtː] | gets / got |
| **söt** [søːt] | **sött** [sœtː] | sweet (with *en* / *ett* words) |
| **blå** [bloː] | **blått** [blɔtː] | blue (with *en* / *ett* words) |

The Swedish letters **å**, **ä** and **ö** are not interchangeable spellings or decorative versions of **a** and **o**. A different letter can signal a different word, so learn the vowel as part of each word. Some regional accents have noticeably different vowel qualities, and some speakers may use more open or more closed versions than the IPA here. Those are normal variations; aim first to keep the three vowel categories and the long-short difference clear.
