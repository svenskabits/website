Swedish **å**, **ä** and **ö** are three distinct vowel letters. They are separate letters—not accents on **a** or **o**—and they can change a word's meaning. In the Swedish alphabet, they come after **z**, in the order **å, ä, ö**.

## How the vowels sound

These are broad descriptions of common standard Swedish pronunciation in central Sweden. The exact vowel quality varies between speakers and regions.

| Letter | Long vowel | Short vowel | Starting point for your mouth |
|---|---|---|---|
| **å** | [oː] | [ɔ] | Round your lips; keep the tongue toward the back. |
| **ä** | [ɛː] | [ɛ] | Keep your lips unrounded and your tongue toward the front. |
| **ö** | [øː] | [œ] | Round your lips, but keep the tongue toward the front. |

The symbol **[ː]** means that the vowel is long. The other symbols are IPA, a system for writing speech sounds; they are not English spellings. English does not have an exact equivalent of Swedish **ö**. A useful way to begin is to shape your tongue as for **ä**, then round your lips. For **å**, round your lips with your tongue farther back. These are starting points, not exact instructions for every speaker.

## Long and short vowels

In a stressed syllable, Swedish usually has either a long vowel followed by a short consonant, or a short vowel followed by a long consonant. A doubled consonant is held longer; it is not said twice with a pause. In spelling, a vowel before one consonant or at the end of a word is often long, while a vowel before a doubled consonant or a consonant group is often short. These are useful clues, not rules without exceptions.

| Long vowel | Short vowel | What changes |
|---|---|---|
| **får** — gets; sheep [foːr] | **fått** — got [fɔtː] | The vowel is long in **får**; the **t** is long in **fått**. |
| **söt** — sweet [søːt] | **sött** — sweet, neuter form [sœtː] | The adjective changes form, and so do the vowel and final consonant lengths. |
| **blå** — blue [bloː] | **blått** — blue, neuter form [blɔtː] | The vowel is short before the doubled **tt**. |

Other examples show the spelling clues: **gå** (to go) has a long **å**, while **åtta** (eight) has a short **å** before **tt**; **äta** (to eat) has a long **ä**, while **äpple** (apple) has a short **ä** before **pp**; **snö** (snow) has a long **ö**, while **höst** (autumn) has a short **ö** before a consonant group. Word structure and spelling history can affect the pattern, so learn the vowel length with each word. The contrast is clearest in stressed syllables; unstressed vowels are less prominent.

## Practise the three vowel qualities

- **Å**: **gå** (to go), **år** (year), **blå** (blue), **fått** (got), **åtta** (eight). Round your lips and keep the tongue farther back.
- **Ä**: **äta** (to eat), **här** (here), **rätt** (right; correct), **lätt** (easy; light). Keep your lips unrounded and the tongue toward the front.
- **Ö**: **öl** (beer), **öga** (eye), **söt** (sweet), **mössa** (hat), **född** (born). Round your lips while keeping the tongue toward the front.

Say one word slowly, then at a natural pace. Notice both the vowel quality and what follows it. In **å**, the lips are rounded and the tongue is farther back; in **ö**, the lips are rounded but the tongue is farther forward; in **ä**, the lips are not rounded. Keep the three qualities distinct, and listen for whether the stressed vowel or the following consonant is long.

Regional accents and individual speakers may use more open or more closed vowel qualities than these broad descriptions suggest. Such variation is normal. Learn a clear standard pronunciation as a starting point, but do not assume that every speaker's vowels will sound identical.
