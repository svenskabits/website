Swedish has a consonant commonly called the **sj-sound**. It is a voiceless sound made by pushing air through a narrow space in the mouth. The exact sound varies noticeably among speakers and regions. It is often made farther back in the mouth than English **sh** in *ship*, so English **sh** can be a rough starting point for some speakers, but it is not a universal match.

## Common spellings

Several spellings can represent the sj-sound. These are useful patterns, not guarantees for every word:

| Spelling | Examples | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **sj** | *sjö*, *sju*, *sjunga* | lake; seven; to sing |
| **skj** | *skjorta*, *skjuta* | shirt; to shoot |
| **stj** | *stjärna*, *stjäl* | star; steals |
| **sk** before **e, i, y, ä, ö** | *sked*, *skina*, *skylt*, *skära*, *skön* | spoon; to shine; sign; to cut; lovely |

In the last pattern, **sk** usually has the sj-sound before one of these front-vowel letters. Compare **k** before the same letters: it commonly has a different soft sound, heard in *kök* (kitchen) and *kyrka* (church). The spelling **sk** does not simply sound like an **s** followed by a hard **k** in words such as *sked*.

## Listen to the sound in words

The spelling does not tell you exactly where in the mouth to make the sound. Listen to a Swedish speaker and notice that the air continues through the consonant rather than stopping as it does for **k** or **t**. Keep the sound voiceless; do not add a vowel before or after it.

- *Sju sjömän sjunger.* — Seven sailors are singing.
- *Stjärnan syns.* — The star is visible.
- *Jag vill ha en skjorta.* — I want a shirt.
- *Skeden ligger på bordet.* — The spoon is on the table.

## Regional and speaker variation

There is no single mouth position or exact sound quality used by everyone in Sweden. The sj-sound may be farther forward or farther back, and its quality can be quite different between regions and individual speakers. Some versions may sound somewhat like English **sh**; others have a more noticeable back-of-the-mouth quality. These are normal variations. Aim for a clear, consistent voiceless sound and learn to recognize different speakers rather than trying to copy only one version.

The sj-sound is also distinct from the soft sound commonly spelled **tj** or **k** before **e, i, y, ä, ö**. These are different sounds in standard Swedish, although their exact realization varies by region and speaker. For example, *kär* (dear) begins with the soft **k** sound, while *själ* (soul) begins with the sj-sound. Listen for the difference, but remember that speakers do not all pronounce it in exactly the same way.

## A spelling caution

The patterns above cover common Swedish words, but spelling alone is not a perfect pronunciation guide. Loanwords, names, and some individual words may follow other patterns, and pronunciations can differ by speaker. When a word is unfamiliar, use a recording or a reliable pronunciation reference rather than assuming that every **sk** or **ch** has the sj-sound.
