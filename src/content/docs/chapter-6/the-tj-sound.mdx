Swedish has a consonant often called the **tj-sound** or **tje-sound**. In standard Swedish in Sweden, it is usually pronounced [ɕ]. This is a voiceless, hiss-like sound: air keeps flowing through a narrow space near the front of the mouth. The symbol [ɕ] is from the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA).

The sound is close to English **sh** in *ship*, but it is usually made farther forward in the mouth and is not exactly the same. Keep it as one consonant; do not pronounce the **t** and **j** in **tj** as two separate sounds.

## Common spellings

| Spelling | Examples | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **tj** | *tjugo*, *tjock*, *tjej*, *tjäna* | twenty; thick/fat; girl; to earn / serve |
| **k** before **e, i, y, ä, ö** | *kedja*, *kyrka*, *köpa*, *känna*, *kök* | chain; church; to buy; to feel / know; kitchen |
| **kj** | *kjol* | skirt |

The vowels **e, i, y, ä, ö** are called front or “soft” vowels. In many everyday Swedish words, **k** before one of these letters has the tj-sound. Before **a, o, u, å**, **k** normally keeps its hard sound, as in *kaka* (cookie), *komma* (to come), *kul* (fun), and *kål* (cabbage). Compare *kök* (kitchen), with the tj-sound, and *kok* (boil), with a hard **k**.

The spelling **kj** is uncommon; *kjol* is a useful everyday example. In a few loanwords, other spellings occur too. For example, **c** has the tj-sound in *cembalo* (harpsichord). These unusual spellings are best learned word by word.

## Listen for the difference

The tj-sound is different from both a hard **k** and the Swedish sj-sound. A hard **k** briefly stops the air; the tj-sound lets air hiss through. The sj-sound is also a continuous hiss, but is generally made farther back in the mouth in standard Swedish in Sweden.

- *Tjugo kronor.* — Twenty kronor.
- *Hon köper en kjol.* — She is buying a skirt.
- *Kyrkan är nära.* — The church is nearby.
- *Jag känner en tjej.* — I know a girl.

Notice that the words *tjugo*, *köper*, *kjol*, *kyrkan*, and *känner* begin with or contain the same kind of soft consonant, even though they are spelled differently.

## Variation

The common pronunciation in Sweden is [ɕ]. Some speakers, including speakers in Finland Swedish and some regional varieties in Sweden, pronounce it with a brief **t**-like beginning, [t͡ɕ]. This is a normal variation. Aim for a clear, consistent sound and expect to hear both versions.

The soft-**k** pattern is very common, but spelling is not a perfect guide in every name or loanword. A few words, including *kex* (biscuit/cracker), have pronunciations that vary between speakers and regions. When you meet an unfamiliar word, check its pronunciation rather than assuming every **k** before a front vowel has the tj-sound.
