In English, **the** comes before a noun: *the car*. In Swedish, the noun usually gets an ending instead: **en bil → bilen** (*a car → the car*). The ending depends on whether the noun is an **en-word** or an **ett-word**, and on how the noun ends.

## Choose the ending

| Noun type | Noun ends in a consonant | Noun ends in a vowel |
|---|---|---|
| **en-word** | add **-en**: **en bil → bilen** (a car → the car) | add **-n**: **en flicka → flickan** (a girl → the girl) |
| **ett-word** | add **-et**: **ett hus → huset** (a house → the house) | add **-t**: **ett äpple → äpplet** (an apple → the apple) |

The ending does not change the noun's en- or ett-group. Learn the group and the definite form together:

| Indefinite singular | Definite singular | English |
|---|---|---|
| **en stol** | **stolen** | a chair; the chair |
| **en gata** | **gatan** | a street; the street |
| **ett bord** | **bordet** | a table; the table |
| **ett piano** | **pianot** | a piano; the piano |

## Spelling changes

The ending is attached to the noun, but the spelling can change slightly:

- After a short stressed vowel, a final consonant is often doubled: **en katt → katten** (a cat → the cat), **ett rum → rummet** (a room → the room).
- Some nouns ending in an unstressed **-el** or **-er** drop the **e** before the ending: **en nyckel → nyckeln** (a key → the key), **en meter → metern** (a metre → the metre).
- Some nouns have a less predictable form or a changed stem. For example, **en man → mannen** (a man → the man) and **ett öga → ögat** (an eye → the eye).

These are useful patterns, but not a way to predict every noun. When you learn a noun, learn its definite form too.

## When to use the definite form

Use the definite form when you mean a specific thing that the listener can identify. A common pattern is to introduce something with an indefinite noun and then refer to it with a definite noun:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **Jag köper en kanelbulle.** | I'm buying a cinnamon bun. |
| **Var är kanelbullen?** | Where is the cinnamon bun? |
| **Jag ser ett äpple.** | I see an apple. |
| **Äpplet är rött.** | The apple is red. |

You can also use the definite form when the situation already makes the thing clear: **Kan du öppna dörren?** (Can you open the door?)

## Definite nouns with other words

With **den här** (this) and **det här** (this), use the definite noun form:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **den här bilen** | this car |
| **det här huset** | this house |
| **Jag tycker om den här låten.** | I like this song. |

When an adjective comes before a definite noun, Swedish normally uses **den** with an en-word or **det** with an ett-word, the adjective form ending in **-a**, and the definite noun ending:

| Swedish | English |
|---|---|
| **den röda bilen** | the red car |
| **det lilla huset** | the small house |
| **Jag vill köpa den röda bilen.** | I want to buy the red car. |

The noun does not usually take the definite ending after a possessive word such as **min** (my), **din** (your), or **hans** (his): **min bil** (my car), not *min bilen*. The possessive already shows which car you mean.

## A quick reminder

For a single definite noun, choose the ending by checking both the noun group and its final sound/spelling: **en + consonant: -en**, **en + vowel: -n**, **ett + consonant: -et**, **ett + vowel: -t**. Attach the ending to the noun, and remember that some nouns also change their spelling.
