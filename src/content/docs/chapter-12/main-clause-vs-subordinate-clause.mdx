A **clause** is a group of words built around a verb. A main clause can stand on its own as a sentence. A subordinate clause depends on another clause: it may give a reason, time, condition, or extra information, but it cannot normally stand alone as a complete sentence.

> Jag arbetar hemifrån.
> *I work from home.*
>
> eftersom jag arbetar hemifrån
> *because I work from home*

The first clause is a complete statement. The second leaves us waiting for the rest of the thought:

> Jag trivs bra eftersom jag arbetar hemifrån.
> *I like it because I work from home.*

## Main clauses: the finite verb is in position two

In an ordinary Swedish main clause, the **finite verb**—the verb marked for tense, or the first verb in a verb phrase—comes in the second position. This is called the **V2 rule**. “Position” means a sentence element, not necessarily a single word: an entire time or place phrase can come first.

| First element | Finite verb | Rest of the clause |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Jag | arbetar | på ett kontor. |
| I | work | in an office. |
| I dag | arbetar | jag på kontoret. |
| Today | I work | at the office. |
| På kontoret | arbetar | jag varje dag. |
| At the office | I work | every day. |

When something other than the subject comes first, the subject usually follows the finite verb. This is **inversion**:

> I morgon ska jag träffa min chef.
> *Tomorrow I will meet my manager.*

The same pattern applies when a main clause follows a subordinate clause:

> När mötet är slut går vi och äter lunch.
> *When the meeting is over, we will go and have lunch.*

The subordinate clause *När mötet är slut* takes the first position in the whole sentence. The main clause therefore begins with the finite verb *går*, followed by the subject *vi*.

### Negation and sentence adverbs in main clauses

In a main clause, words such as *inte* (not), *alltid* (always), and *kanske* (perhaps) usually come **after the finite verb**. With a verb phrase, they usually come after the first verb:

> Jag arbetar inte på fredagar.
> *I don't work on Fridays.*
>
> Hon har aldrig varit i Göteborg.
> *She has never been to Gothenburg.*
>
> Vi kan kanske ta tåget.
> *Perhaps we can take the train.*

## Subordinate clauses: the subject comes before the finite verb

A subordinate clause often starts with a **subordinator**, such as *att* (that), *eftersom* (because), *när* (when), or *om* (if/whether). After the subordinator, the usual order is:

**subordinator + subject + sentence adverb + finite verb + rest**

> … eftersom **jag inte arbetar** på fredagar.
> *… because **I don't work** on Fridays.*
>
> … att **hon alltid kommer** i tid.
> *… that **she always arrives** on time.*

Unlike in a main clause, *inte* and similar sentence adverbs usually come **before the finite verb** in a subordinate clause. This is a useful check when choosing the word order: **in a subordinate clause, the adverb comes before the verb**.

> Jag vet att han inte kan komma.
> *I know that he can't come.*
>
> De stannade hemma eftersom de var trötta.
> *They stayed home because they were tired.*

The verb phrase stays together after the adverb: *inte kan komma* (“cannot come”), with the finite verb *kan* after *inte*.

Common subordinators include:

| Subordinator | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *att* | that | Jag tror att hon är hemma. — *I think that she is at home.* |
| *eftersom* | because | Vi tar bussen eftersom det regnar. — *We are taking the bus because it is raining.* |
| *när* | when | Ring mig när du kommer hem. — *Call me when you get home.* |
| *om* | if / whether | Jag vet inte om han har tid. — *I don't know if he has time.* |
| *fast* | although / even though | Hon gick till jobbet fast hon var sjuk. — *She went to work even though she was ill.* |
| *innan* | before | Stäng fönstret innan du går. — *Close the window before you leave.* |
| *medan* | while | Jag lagar mat medan barnen leker. — *I'm cooking while the children play.* |
| *så att* | so that | Tala långsamt så att alla förstår. — *Speak slowly so that everyone understands.* |

The subordinator may be followed by an entire clause, including its own subject and verb. A subordinate clause can come before or after the main clause:

> Eftersom det regnar tar vi bussen.
> *Because it is raining, we are taking the bus.*
>
> Vi tar bussen eftersom det regnar.
> *We are taking the bus because it is raining.*

When the subordinate clause comes first, the main clause still follows the V2 rule: *tar* is first in the main clause, then *vi*.

## Direct and indirect questions

A direct question is a main clause. In a yes/no question, the finite verb comes before the subject. In a direct question with a question word, the question word is followed by the finite verb:

> Kommer hon i dag?
> *Is she coming today?*
>
> Var bor han?
> *Where does he live?*

An **indirect question** is embedded in another clause, so it has subordinate word order: subject before finite verb. The question word stays at the beginning of the embedded question. Yes/no questions commonly use *om* (“if/whether”):

> Jag undrar om hon kommer i dag.
> *I wonder if she is coming today.*
>
> Vet du var han bor?
> *Do you know where he lives?*

Compare the direct question *Var bor han?* with the indirect question *var han bor*. Do not use the direct-question order inside the subordinate clause.

## Relative clauses

A relative clause gives more information about a noun. It is subordinate and often begins with *som* (“who,” “which,” or “that”). Use subordinate word order inside it:

> Jag har en kollega som inte dricker kaffe.
> *I have a colleague who doesn't drink coffee.*
>
> Det här är rapporten som chefen skrev.
> *This is the report that the manager wrote.*

In the first example, *inte* comes before the finite verb *dricker*. In the second, *som* is the object of *skrev*, so the subject *chefen* comes before the verb.

## Coordinated main clauses

Two main clauses can be joined with a coordinating conjunction such as *och* (and), *men* (but), or *eller* (or). Each clause keeps main-clause word order, including V2:

> Jag sökte jobbet, men jag fick inget svar.
> *I applied for the job, but I didn't get an answer.*
>
> Hon lagar middag och barnen dukar bordet.
> *She is making dinner and the children are setting the table.*

Do not confuse these with a subordinate clause introduced by a subordinator such as *eftersom* or *när*: that clause uses subordinate word order.

## A quick way to check

1. Find the clause's finite verb.
2. Decide whether the clause is a main clause or a subordinate clause. A subordinator, an indirect question, or a relative clause often signals a subordinate clause.
3. In a main clause, check that the finite verb is in the second position; *inte* usually follows it.
4. In a subordinate clause, check that the subject comes before the finite verb; *inte* usually comes before that verb.

> I dag **kommer** hon **inte** eftersom hon **inte mår** bra.
> *Today she is not coming because she is not feeling well.*

The main clause starts with *I dag*, so its finite verb *kommer* follows in second position. The subordinate clause starts with *eftersom*, and *inte* comes before its finite verb *mår*.
