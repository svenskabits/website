A relative clause adds information about a noun or pronoun. In English, it often begins with *who*, *which*, or *that*. In Swedish, the most common relative word is **som**. It does not change for gender or number.

> Jag har en vän **som bor i Göteborg**.  
> I have a friend **who lives in Gothenburg**.

The noun that the relative clause describes is called its **antecedent**. Here, *en vän* is the antecedent of *som bor i Göteborg*.

## Som as the subject

When **som** does the action in the relative clause, it is the subject. Keep **som** in the clause; it cannot normally be left out in this use.

| Swedish | English |
| --- | --- |
| Kvinnan **som arbetar här** talar tre språk. | The woman **who works here** speaks three languages. |
| Jag söker ett jobb **som passar mig**. | I am looking for a job **that suits me**. |
| Vi bor i ett hus **som ligger nära stationen**. | We live in a house **that is near the station**. |

The verb agrees with the subject inside the relative clause, not with **som** as a special form. Swedish verbs do not change for singular and plural:

> Jag har två kollegor **som arbetar hemifrån**.  
> I have two colleagues **who work from home**.

## Som as the object

When **som** is the object of a verb in the relative clause, it can often be omitted, especially in everyday Swedish. The subject and verb remain:

| With **som** | Without **som** | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Filmen **som vi såg** var bra. | Filmen **vi såg** var bra. | The film **that we saw** was good. |
| Personen **som jag träffade** var trevlig. | Personen **jag träffade** var trevlig. | The person **who I met** was nice. |

Keeping **som** is always a clear choice. Do not omit it when it is the subject:

> Boken **som ligger på bordet** är min.  
> The book **that is lying on the table** is mine.

Here, *boken* is the subject of *ligger*, so **som** is needed. Compare:

> Boken **(som) jag läser** är spännande.  
> The book **that I am reading** is exciting.

Here, *jag* is the subject and **som** is the object of *läser*, so it can be omitted.

## Word order and inte

A relative clause is a subordinate clause. Its word order is like other Swedish subordinate clauses: the subject comes before the finite verb, and **inte** normally comes before the verb.

> Jag har en chef **som inte stressar upp sig**.  
> I have a manager **who does not get stressed**.

> Det är ett företag **som jag inte känner till**.  
> It is a company **that I do not know about**.

In the first example, **som** is the subject, followed by **inte** and the verb. In the second, **jag** is the subject, **som** is the object, and **inte** comes before the verb.

## Relative clauses with a preposition

When the relative clause includes a preposition, the preposition usually stays at the end of the clause in everyday Swedish. **Som** (or nothing, when it is an object) comes before the subject:

> Det är jobbet **som jag trivs med**.  
> That is the job **that I enjoy**. (Literally: “that I thrive with.”)

> Här är kollegan **(som) jag pratade med**.  
> Here is the colleague **who I spoke with**.

The preposition still belongs with the verb or expression: *trivas med* (“enjoy / feel comfortable with”), *prata med* (“talk to”). In more formal language, a preposition can be placed before a relative pronoun such as **vilken**, but that is less common in everyday speech.

## Den, det, or de som

The combinations **den som**, **det som**, and **de som** can mean “the person/one who,” “what/the thing that,” and “the people/those who.” The form agrees with what it refers to:

| Swedish | English |
| --- | --- |
| **Den som** kommer först får välja plats. | **The person who** arrives first gets to choose a seat. |
| **Det som** står på bordet är till dig. | **What** is on the table is for you. |
| **De som** arbetar här kan hjälpa dig. | **Those who** work here can help you. |

In **den/det/de som** expressions, **som** is generally the subject of its clause, so it is not omitted.

## Choosing a comma

Do not put a comma before a relative clause that identifies which person or thing you mean:

> Jag behöver en lägenhet **som har balkong**.  
> I need an apartment **that has a balcony**.

The clause tells us which kind of apartment is needed. A comma may be used when the clause adds extra, non-essential information about an already identified person or thing:

> Min syster, **som bor i Uppsala**, kommer på lördag.  
> My sister, **who lives in Uppsala**, is coming on Saturday.

The commas show that the information about where she lives is an aside, rather than information needed to identify which sister is meant.

## Do not confuse relative som with other uses

**Som** has other meanings too. In a relative clause, it refers back to a noun or pronoun:

> Jag gillar restaurangen **som ligger vid torget**.  
> I like the restaurant **that is by the square**.

But **som** can also mean “as” or “like,” without introducing a relative clause:

> Hon arbetar **som lärare**.  
> She works **as a teacher**.

> Han springer **som en vind**.  
> He runs **like the wind**.
