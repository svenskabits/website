A cleft sentence lets you put special focus on one part of a sentence. Swedish often does this with **det är … som** or **det var … som**. The focused part comes after *det är/var*, and the rest of the message follows in a clause introduced by *som* (or, in some cases, without *som*).

Compare the neutral sentence with the cleft sentence:

| Neutral sentence | Cleft sentence |
| --- | --- |
| *Sara ringde igår.* — Sara called yesterday. | *Det var Sara som ringde igår.* — It was Sara who called yesterday. |
| *Jag köpte den blå jackan.* — I bought the blue jacket. | *Det var den blå jackan jag köpte.* — It was the blue jacket I bought. |

The cleft version answers an implied question such as “Who called?” or “Which jacket did you buy?” In speech, the focused part normally carries the strongest stress.

## The basic pattern

The usual pattern is:

> **det + är/var + focused part + (som) + the rest of the clause**

Use *det är* for a present-time identification or a general statement, and *det var* when the identifying event or situation is in the past. The verb in the following clause has its own tense; it does not have to match *är* or *var*.

| Focus | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Person | *Det är Amir som ansvarar för projektet.* — Amir is the one who is responsible for the project. |
| Thing | *Det var den här rapporten som chefen bad om.* — This was the report the manager asked for. |
| Place | *Det var på biblioteket vi träffades.* — It was at the library that we met. |
| Time | *Det är på fredagar som vi brukar ha möte.* — Fridays are when we usually have a meeting. |
| Reason or manner | *Det var därför hon tackade nej.* — That was why she turned it down. |

## What can be focused?

You can focus many different parts of a sentence: the subject, object, place, time, or another phrase. The rest of the clause keeps its normal word order. If the focused phrase is removed, the following clause still contains the other parts of the original sentence.

**A subject can be focused before *som*.** The subject remains the subject of the clause:

- *Det var min syster som hittade nycklarna.* — It was my sister who found the keys.
- *Det är företaget som betalar resan.* — The company is paying for the trip.

**An object can be focused.** Swedish commonly leaves out *som* before the clause when the focused phrase is its object. Including *som* is also possible in many such sentences:

- *Det var den här boken jag läste på semestern.* — This was the book I read on vacation.
- *Det var den här boken som jag läste på semestern.* — This was the book that I read on vacation.
- *Det är dig jag väntar på.* — You are the one I’m waiting for.

The preposition stays with the clause: *väntar på* means “wait for.” In formal writing, a pronoun used as an object takes its object form, as in *dig* above.

**A time or place phrase can also be focused.** *Som* is often omitted in these examples, though it can be included:

- *Det var i Malmö (som) de först träffades.* — It was in Malmö that they first met.
- *Det är nästa vecka (som) beslutet ska fattas.* — It is next week that the decision will be made.

The focused element can also be a negative or limiting expression:

- *Det var inte förrän i juni som vi fick svar.* — It was not until June that we got an answer.
- *Det är bara Anna som känner till lösenordet.* — Anna is the only one who knows the password.

## *Som* and its omission

Use *som* especially clearly when the focused element is the subject of the following clause: *Det var Karin som ringde.* With an object, time, place, or similar phrase, *som* is often optional, particularly in everyday Swedish: *Det var Karin jag ringde* (“Karin was the one I called”). When in doubt, keeping *som* is a safe and natural choice in a standard cleft sentence.

Do not confuse this optional *som* with *som* inside the clause as a relative word referring to a noun. For example, *som* in *Det är den läkare som jag träffade* (“That is the doctor whom I met”) belongs to the noun phrase *den läkare som jag träffade*; the sentence is not necessarily a cleft. Context and stress help make the difference clear.

## Tense, questions, and negation

The verb *är/var* identifies the focused part. The verb in the following clause describes the event or state and uses the tense it needs:

- *Det är Maria som har skrivit rapporten.* — Maria is the one who has written the report.
- *Det var Maria som skrev rapporten.* — Maria was the one who wrote the report.
- *Det var i går som hon skulle ha ringt.* — It was yesterday that she was supposed to call.

In a yes/no question, put *är* or *var* before *det*:

- *Är det du som har bokat rummet?* — Are you the one who booked the room?
- *Var det på den här adressen ni bodde?* — Was it at this address that you lived?

Negation can be placed in different parts of the sentence, depending on what is being denied:

- *Det var inte Lina som ringde, utan hennes chef.* — It wasn’t Lina who called, but her manager.
- *Det var Lina som inte kunde komma.* — Lina was the one who could not come.

In the first example, *inte* denies the identification of the caller. In the second, Lina is identified as the person, and it is her inability to come that is denied.

## A related pattern: *det som … är*

Swedish also has a related pattern that starts with *det som* and puts the important information at the end. It is sometimes called a pseudo-cleft. Here *det som* means “what” or “the thing that”:

- *Det som behövs är mer tid.* — What is needed is more time.
- *Det jag uppskattar mest är att alla hjälper till.* — What I appreciate most is that everyone helps out.

This is different from the usual *det är … som* cleft: compare *Det är mer tid som behövs* (“It is more time that is needed”) with *Det som behövs är mer tid* (“What is needed is more time”). The first focuses *mer tid* before the clause; the second introduces the topic first and gives the answer at the end.

## Do not mistake other uses of *det är* for clefts

Not every sentence with *det är* is a cleft. *Det är svårt att förklara* (“It is difficult to explain”) is not a cleft: *det* anticipates the idea expressed by *att förklara*. Also distinguish a cleft from an ordinary existential sentence: *Det finns en kvinna som arbetar här* means “There is a woman who works here.” By contrast, *Det är en kvinna som arbetar här* focuses *en kvinna*—for example, as an answer to “Who works here?” A cleft has a clear focused part, often understood as an answer to a question or a correction.
