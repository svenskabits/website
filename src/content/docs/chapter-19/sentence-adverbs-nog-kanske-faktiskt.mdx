**Nog**, **kanske** and **faktiskt** are adverbs that can comment on the likelihood or truth of a whole statement. They do not describe an action in the way an adverb such as *snabbt* (“quickly”) does. Instead, they show how the speaker views the information: as a likely guess, a possibility, or something that is true despite expectations.

Their English translations depend on context. In particular, **nog** does not have one fixed equivalent such as “surely.”

## Nog: a likely conclusion or reassuring expectation

In a statement, **nog** often presents something as probable based on what the speaker knows or expects. It can also make a prediction sound reassuring or confident:

| Swedish | English |
| --- | --- |
| Hon kommer nog lite senare. | She'll probably come a little later. |
| Det går nog bra. | It'll probably be fine. |
| Du har nog rätt. | You're probably right. |

Depending on the context and intonation, **nog** can sound more confident than “probably,” closer to “I expect” or “I'm sure.” It does not normally mean that the speaker has absolute certainty:

> De hittar nog hotellet. — They should be able to find the hotel. / They'll probably find the hotel.

At the beginning of a main clause, **nog** can receive stronger emphasis and suggest that the speaker is confident or willing to insist on a point. The finite verb still follows the fronted adverb:

> Nog blir det bättre i morgon. — Surely it will be better tomorrow. / It will get better tomorrow, I'm sure.

This fronted pattern is more emphatic than the ordinary *Det blir nog bättre i morgon* (“It will probably be better tomorrow”).

Do not confuse sentence-adverb **nog** with **nog** meaning “enough,” which typically describes an amount or degree:

> Vi har nog med tid. — We have enough time.

## Kanske: a possibility

**Kanske** means “maybe” or “perhaps.” It presents an event or claim as possible without saying how likely it is:

| Swedish | English |
| --- | --- |
| Jag kanske stannar hemma i kväll. | Maybe I'll stay home tonight. |
| Hon kommer kanske efter lunch. | She may come after lunch. |
| Kanske har de redan gått. | Perhaps they have already left. |

In a main clause with the subject first, **kanske** can come either before or after the finite verb:

> Bussen kanske är försenad. — The bus may be delayed.  
> Bussen är kanske försenad. — The bus may be delayed.

The first order is very common in speech. When **kanske** is placed at the start of the clause, the finite verb comes next, before the subject. This is the normal verb-second pattern in a Swedish main clause:

> Kanske är bussen försenad. — Perhaps the bus is delayed.

In a subordinate clause, the subject comes before **kanske**, and **kanske** comes before the finite verb:

> Jag tror att bussen kanske är försenad. — I think the bus may be delayed.

## Faktiskt: surprising or emphatic fact

**Faktiskt** means “actually,” “in fact” or “as a matter of fact.” It presents something as true, often when the listener might not expect it, or when the speaker wants to correct or emphasize a point:

| Swedish | English |
| --- | --- |
| Jag tyckte faktiskt om filmen. | I actually liked the film. |
| Hon har faktiskt redan svarat. | She has in fact already replied. |
| Det är faktiskt inte så svårt. | It really isn't that difficult. |

The word does not always signal disagreement or surprise. The surrounding conversation and the speaker's emphasis determine whether it sounds like a correction, a reminder or simple emphasis.

At the beginning of a main clause, **faktiskt** is followed by the finite verb, with the subject after it:

> Faktiskt känner jag honom sedan länge. — In fact, I've known him for a long time.

In a subordinate clause, **faktiskt** comes after the subject and before the finite verb:

> Hon sa att hon faktiskt kände honom sedan länge. — She said that she had in fact known him for a long time.

## Position in the clause

In a main clause, a sentence adverb often comes after the finite verb. The finite verb is the verb that carries tense, or the first verb in a verb phrase:

| Swedish | English |
| --- | --- |
| Hon kommer kanske i kväll. | She may come tonight. |
| Hon har faktiskt ringt. | She has actually called. |
| Hon har nog glömt tiden. | She has probably lost track of time. |

With a negative sentence, the adverb usually comes before **inte** when it follows the finite verb:

> De har kanske inte sett meddelandet. — Maybe they haven't seen the message.  
> Jag har faktiskt inte träffat honom. — I actually haven't met him.  
> Det blir nog inte klart i dag. — It probably won't be ready today.

**Kanske** also commonly appears between the subject and finite verb in a subject-first main clause, as shown above (*De kanske inte har sett meddelandet* — “Maybe they haven't seen the message”). This order is not available in the same way for **nog** or **faktiskt**; use the position after the finite verb for those in ordinary subject-first statements.

In a subordinate clause introduced by words such as **att** (“that”), the usual order is subject + sentence adverb + finite verb:

> Jag vet att hon kanske inte kommer. — I know that she may not come.  
> De sa att de faktiskt hade glömt biljetterna. — They said that they had actually forgotten the tickets.  
> Jag tror att det nog blir sol i morgon. — I think it will probably be sunny tomorrow.

When one of these adverbs is placed first in a main clause, the finite verb follows it and the subject comes after the verb. Compare:

> Kanske kommer hon i morgon. — Perhaps she'll come tomorrow.  
> Faktiskt kommer hon i morgon. — In fact, she is coming tomorrow.

The fronted order gives the adverb extra prominence. It does not apply in a subordinate clause, where the subject remains before the finite verb.

## Choosing between them

The three adverbs express different attitudes toward a statement:

| Adverb | Speaker's stance | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **nog** | The speaker thinks it is likely or expects it to be true. | *Han kommer nog.* — He'll probably come. |
| **kanske** | The speaker presents it as one possibility. | *Han kanske kommer.* — Maybe he'll come. |
| **faktiskt** | The speaker asserts it as a fact, often against an expectation. | *Han kommer faktiskt.* — He actually is coming. |

These meanings can overlap in translation, but the Swedish words are not interchangeable. **Nog** and **kanske** both soften a prediction, but **nog** presents it as the speaker's likely expectation, while **kanske** simply leaves open the possibility. **Faktiskt** asserts the information rather than marking it as uncertain.
